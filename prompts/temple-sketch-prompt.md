# Temple line-drawing prompt (architectural esquisse)

Paste the whole block below alongside the temple photograph. The line marked
PER-TEMPLE is the only part that changes between temples; fill in the column
counts read off the reference photos before running.

---

Convert the attached photograph of this temple into a loose architectural esquisse: a designer's freehand concept sketch in pure black ink line on a pure white background, the building centered with generous white margin on all sides. Reproduce this exact building faithfully from the photograph: its massing, proportions, tower and spire configuration, roof shapes, window placement and rhythm, entrance, and the same viewing angle as the photo. Every mark is a pen stroke; nothing is filled, shaded, toned, or hatched. The entire drawing is boldly inked with a broad felt-tip or brush pen rather than a fine liner, so even the lightest lines are substantial, clearly visible marks and the heaviest read as thick, weighty bands, and the whole sketch prints strong and graphic rather than delicate.

Proportions are taken from the photograph and this paragraph outranks every composition rule later in this prompt. Before drawing, register the building's key ratios directly from the photo: how many times taller than the main walls the tower and spire stand, how far above the main roofline the topmost element rises, and how wide the whole building spans relative to the wall height. Carry those exact ratios into the drawing unchanged. Whatever is tall in the photograph is equally tall in the drawing; whatever is slender stays equally slender; whatever is long and low stays long and low. Towers and spires are the most commonly distorted elements: never shorten a tower, thicken a spire shaft, or make any vertical element stubbier, blockier, or more compact than the photograph shows. If anything, err toward the tower reading slightly taller and thinner than the photo, never shorter and wider. Never compress the building vertically or squash its massing to fit the frame, to leave room for margins, or to suit the pen; if space is tight, scale the entire drawing down uniformly and keep every ratio intact. Any style reference images govern stroke character and line weight only; every proportion, ratio, and dimension comes from the photograph alone, and nothing about the building shapes in the reference drawings transfers.

Peaks and points are closed. This rule outranks every other rule in this prompt except the proportion paragraph above. Wherever two lines converge to a point, whether the apex of a spire, steeple, pinnacle, turret, gable, dormer, buttress cap, or finial, the two strokes travel up, meet, and stop exactly at the tip. Nothing whatsoever continues past a peak: no overshoot, no tail, no guide line, no draft line, no construction line, no bar, no mark of any kind above, through, or beside it. Every spire is a clean unbroken triangle rising into blank paper. The sky around and above each spire and tower is completely empty white, and no line passes in front of, behind, or across a spire shaft at any height along its rise.

Overrun every junction where two lines meet and both keep going. Window corners, door corners, wall corners, roof-to-wall junctions, sloped eave meetings, buttress and setback edges, band intersections, feature corners, base corners: at each of these, whether the meeting is square or oblique, each line runs visibly past the meeting point, leaving open overshooting tails, so rectangles read as four long strokes overlapping near their corners rather than closed shapes. This applies only to junctions where lines genuinely pass one another; it never applies to a converging point or apex, which always terminates cleanly. Interior detail lines overrun just as universally as structural ones. Every window corner, door corner, sill end, band, cornice, mullion, and subordinate feature edge crosses and continues past its neighbours in the lightest weight, as small, crisp, crossed tails, dense across the whole facade, so the wall surfaces read as a field of overlapping strokes rather than tidy closed rectangles. The peak rule above governs only the tapering silhouette of spires and pinnacles; it never suppresses these small interior crossings anywhere else on the building, including high on the towers.

Tails that shoot beyond the building's silhouette into the open white are very long and decisive, drawn at the medium weight: a thick, substantial stroke, clearly bolder than the interior detail lines and about half the thickness of the heavy structural outline, laid with the same broad pen and never thinning to a scratch as it travels. These long tails occur at every height of the building, top to bottom: the wall base, the plinth corners, the main wall corners, the horizontal bands, the roof edges, the tower shoulders, the cornices and setbacks high on the towers, and the bases of pinnacles. The longest of them reach out from the silhouette by about a fourth of the building's width, a good number of the others come close to that, and only a few are short stubs, so they read as confident strokes carrying real distance into the white rather than small nicks at the edge. Vertical tails at the wall base drop a comparable distance below the building. Tail lengths vary irregularly with no two the same, and none travels so far that it nears the edge of the page. Each tail continues in the exact direction of the line that made it.

One exception, and it applies only to the spires themselves. Above the springing line where a spire, steeple, or pinnacle begins to taper toward its point, no tails are generated at all: the two converging edges of that cone or pyramid run cleanly to the tip with nothing branching off them, nothing overshooting the tip, and nothing crossing the shaft from either side. Overrun tails elsewhere may be long, but none of them is aimed so as to pass through or over a spire; the tapering portion of every spire sits in untouched white from its springing line to its point.

Line hierarchy, three clearly separated weights in a fixed ratio of roughly 5 : 2.5 : 1. HEAVIEST, five units: the full outer silhouette of the building and every major structural edge, meaning wall corners, roofline, tower and spire edges, the edges where one volume meets another, and the base edge of the walls, all drawn at one uniform heavy weight regardless of direction. These are the thickest marks on the page, about one hundredth of the image width, reading as bold weighty ribbons of ink so the entire structure registers at a glance from across a room. MEDIUM, two and a half units: the overrun tails that extend past the silhouette into the surrounding white space, a full, substantial stroke, half the thickness of the heavy structural outline and well over twice the interior detail lines, never a thin scratch or a tapering hairline. LIGHTEST, one unit: all interior detail lines, meaning window and door openings, horizontal bands, cornice lines, subordinate feature edges, and the overrun tails that stay inside the silhouette; these are the thinnest lines in the drawing but still firm, confident strokes at roughly one five-hundredth of the image width, clearly visible from a distance, never faint hairlines. The three weights must be unmistakably distinct from one another at a glance.

Stroke character: confident, decisive strokes, but never mechanically uniform. Each line varies in thickness along its length, swelling and thinning slightly, or tapering gradually toward its ends, so every stroke has its own organic silhouette, like ink laid by hand with changing pressure. This variation is subtle and never breaks the weight hierarchy: a heavy line at its thinnest point is still clearly heavier than a medium line, and a medium tail at its thinnest point is still clearly heavier than an interior detail line. Not shaky or wobbly, just alive.

Detail rules. Draw window and door openings as simple open outlines in the lightest weight with overshooting corner tails. If the photograph shows a mullion or major dividing bar inside a window, draw it as a single light stroke that may run slightly past the window frame; if the photo shows none, leave the window empty. A few small touches of draftsman's detail are welcome, always in the lightest weight: a short sill stroke beneath each window that runs slightly past the jambs on both sides; a second inner line following the curve of any arched opening or arched window head; cornices and horizontal trim bands drawn as two closely spaced parallel lines rather than one; and along two or three of the heaviest silhouette edges anywhere on the building except the tapering portion of a spire, a second searching stroke laid slightly off the first, as if the hand tested the line before committing. Never add more than this: no textures, no repeated tick marks, no invented ornament the photograph does not show. Never draw fine glazing grids, small individual panes, or resolved tracery. Never draw brick, stone coursing, shingle lines, siding, railing pickets, or any repeating surface texture; all wall and roof surfaces stay empty white. Omit all landscaping: no trees, shrubs, flowers, lawns, paths, fences, lampposts, cars, or people. The bottommost edge of the building, where its walls and plinth terminate, is always drawn, in the heaviest weight, running the full width of the structure: this line belongs to the building itself and is never omitted. Below that edge there is no ground line, horizon line, site line, or shadow of any kind; the building floats on open white paper, and the only marks beneath the wall base are the vertical overrun tails dropping from the building's own corners and edges. No text, lettering, signatures, labels, or watermarks anywhere. Crisp black on white, no paper texture, no border, no background tone.

Counted elements. Wherever the photograph shows a run of repeated structural parts, such as columns in a colonnade or portico, piers, pilasters, bays, arches, or a row of windows, count them in the photograph and draw exactly that many, no more and no fewer. Count each side of an entrance separately, and count what is actually visible from this viewing angle rather than what the building has in total: a column standing behind another, or returning around a corner, still counts if any part of it can be seen, and a column fully hidden by the building's mass is not drawn. Keep the spacing and foreshortening the angle produces, so the near end of a colonnade reads wider apart than the far end; do not space them evenly if the photograph does not. The ban on repeating surface texture stated above covers brick, stone coursing, shingles, siding, and railing pickets only, and never suppresses structural columns, which are always drawn and always counted. This count is a proportion fact taken from the photograph and outranks any impulse to simplify or to fill the available width. PER-TEMPLE: for this photograph, the entrance portico has five columns on each side of the doors, plus one further column set back behind the outermost column on each side; from this viewing angle, draw six columns to the left of the entrance and four to the right.

Column shape. A column is a round shaft, never a flat slab, never a plain rectangle, and never a taper running toward a point. Draw each one as two vertical edges in the lightest weight with clear white between them. Nothing about a column narrows as it rises: the shaft holds an even width or swells very slightly toward the top, and it finishes in a capital that spreads visibly wider than the shaft on both sides, so the whole column reads as growing wider the higher it goes. If the two edges of a column converge as they go up, the drawing is wrong. The rules earlier in this prompt about elements tapering toward a tip govern spires, steeples, pinnacles, and finials only, and have no bearing on any column, pier, or post. Roundness is shown with line alone and never with shading, hatching, tone, or contour strokes laid inside the shaft: close the top and bottom of each shaft, and the capital and the base, with a shallow curved stroke rather than a straight horizontal line, bowing upward where the element sits above the viewer's eye level and downward where it sits below, the way a cylinder reads in line drawing, and let the two shaft edges bow almost imperceptibly outward at mid height rather than running mechanically straight. Where a colonnade recedes, the farther columns keep the same curved terminations, flared capital, and rounded base, only narrower and closer together. If the photograph clearly shows square piers rather than round columns, draw them square with straight horizontal terminations, but the rule against narrowing toward the top still holds.

Each steeple or spire is topped exactly as shown in the photograph, whether a finial, ball, weathervane, standing figure, or plain bare point, and nothing else. The topmost mark of every tower is that element itself; no extra shape, bar, stroke, or line is added at, above, or near the top of any spire, steeple, pinnacle, or tower.

Composition and framing: the building sits squarely in the middle of a square frame, its horizontal center aligned with the center of the image and equal white margin left and right. Vertically, place it so the margin above the topmost spire tip is a little larger than the margin below the lowest hanging tail, giving the towers room to rise. Margins are measured from the outermost overrun tails, not from the walls: every tail, in all four directions, falls comfortably inside the frame with clear white beyond it. Nothing is cropped, clipped, or touches an edge: no spire tip, no finial, no tail end. The building is never pushed to one side, and no part of the composition is weighted or offset for effect. These margins are achieved only by scaling the whole drawing uniformly, never by shortening the tower, compressing the building vertically, or altering any proportion stated above.
