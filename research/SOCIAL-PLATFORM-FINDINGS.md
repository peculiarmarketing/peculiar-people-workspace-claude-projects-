# Social Platform Findings

Peculiar People. Research completed 3 September 2026.

---

## What this is, and how it was done

The question was narrow and practical. Peculiar People has a finished catalogue, a working pipeline, zero orders and no audience. Distribution is the only constraint. So: which social platforms are worth the time of two people who are not full-time creators, and which are not?

That question has been asked before here in a general way. This round asked it platform by platform, on the platform's own terms, and tried hard to answer two things that earlier rounds treated as assumptions. Does breadth actually help, or does it just cost? And what is each platform genuinely unable to do?

Three kinds of evidence were used and kept separate throughout.

**Class A, primary data.** Audience size, age, sex, United States versus global. Pew Research Center's National Public Opinion Reference Survey (5,022 US adults, fielded 5 February to 18 June 2025), DataReportal's Digital 2026 United States report (October 2025 data), and the quarterly filings of Meta, Pinterest, Snap, Reddit, Etsy and Microsoft, read from SEC EDGAR rather than from press coverage. Every number in this report is dated, and United States figures are kept separate from global ones throughout. Where a number could not be traced to a source of this quality, it was thrown out, and several widely quoted marketing statistics were thrown out for exactly that reason.

**Class B, mechanics.** How distribution works, what links do, what the rules say. Platform-owned documentation first: Meta's newsroom and transparency centre, Instagram's creator guidance, TikTok's support pages and both of TikTok Shop's US product policies, Pinterest's business help, YouTube's monetisation policies, Snapchat's business pages, X's open-source ranking code. Then four practitioner videos, watched in full, chosen because they answer questions the documentation does not.

**Class C, first-hand reconnaissance.** I went and looked, logged out, on 3 September 2026. This turned out to be the most valuable part of the research and also the part with the biggest holes, because five of the platforms refuse to show a logged-out visitor anything at all.

**A disclosure about Facebook.** The browser used for this research was already signed in to Evan's personal Facebook account. I did not log in and did not know until I started. All Facebook figures below were read from group search results as that account, read-only. Nothing was joined, opened, posted in or messaged.

**And a gap.** Reddit is blocked to both of my tools by policy. I could not read a single subreddit. Reddit's profile is built from Pew, its SEC filings and one practitioner source, and its audience question is unanswered.

Claims are labelled throughout. **CONFIRMED** means two or more independent sources agree and any number is backed by Class A data. **SINGLE SOURCE** means one source, kept because it is useful, marked because it is one. **CONFLICT** means sources disagree, and my read follows. **UNKNOWN** appears more often than is comfortable, which is the correct outcome when the alternative is a plausible sentence with nothing behind it.

The recommendation is at the end. The reasoning comes first, because the reasoning is what makes the recommendation adjustable when something changes.

## The two questions, answered up front

Both questions have answers that are more interesting than a yes or a no, so they are worth stating before the platform profiles.

### How much does breadth actually matter?

**Cross-posting your own original work to several platforms carries no documented penalty, and the folklore that it does is a misreading of rules that are about something else.**

Meta's originality policy of 13 March 2026 says it deprioritises "content that is duplicative or involves minor edits to another creator's post". Instagram's creator guidance says aggregator accounts posting ten or more copies within thirty days will not appear in recommendations, and that content with visible watermarks from other apps is ineligible. Read the language: every one of those rules describes reposting *somebody else's* work, or carrying *another platform's* watermark. None of them describes a creator uploading their own file natively to a second platform.

A practitioner spent six months testing exactly this, posting the same short-form videos to Reels, TikTok and Shorts, and recommends continuing (Katie Steckly, 15 September 2025, 51,850 views). Her only reason not to cross-post identically is not a penalty at all. It is format fit: B-roll plus trending audio, the classic Instagram formula, does well on Instagram, decently on TikTok and poorly on YouTube Shorts, because Shorts viewers want explanatory content rather than trend content. Erin On Demand corroborates independently that Instagram's best-performing content underperforms on TikTok (6 January 2026).

So distribution is close to free. **What is not free is everything else.**

Each additional platform that merely *receives* an existing asset costs ten to thirty minutes a week. Each additional platform that *demands* its own native behaviour costs two to ten hours a week. That is the visible cost. Three invisible costs matter more.

The first is the comment obligation. The Posting Rota commits to replying to every comment and message personally, and names that as the single thing separating people who get value from social from people who do not. That obligation scales with platforms, not with posts. Six inboxes is six inboxes.

The second is judgement. Every platform has its own norms and its own failure modes, and you only discover them from inside. This study found that the #ldstemple tag on TikTok is roughly half ex-Mormon and "cult" framing content while the same tag on Instagram has none of it. That is not a thing you learn from a dashboard.

The third is the one that actually decides it. **Breadth costs the ability to learn.** The Posting Rota's week-four review depends on ranking twenty posts by saves and shares to find the one pillar worth splintering hardest. Spread the same twenty posts across six platforms and each platform yields three or four data points, which is not enough to read anything at all. A brand with zero orders needs the reading more than it needs the reach.

**So: two platforms done properly, with the assets also distributed to three or four more that cost minutes.** That is not a compromise between the two options in the question. It is a different answer, and it is the one the evidence supports. Produce for one place. Distribute to several. Only count the ones you are actually willing to read.

### Is "be everywhere" a reach argument or a credibility argument?

They are genuinely different and they lead to different amounts of work, so they should be decided separately.

The reach argument says an account exists to put content in front of strangers. It costs hours every week forever and only makes sense where the audience is and the format fits.

The credibility argument says an account exists so that a buyer who looks the brand up finds something rather than nothing. It costs an hour once. For this brand it matters unusually much, because there are no reviews, no testimonials and no photograph of a real person wearing the product. A stranger asked to pay $44.99 with no social proof will look. An empty result is a reason not to buy.

A practitioner named the behaviour exactly: "Nine times out of 10, a brand is going to go to your Instagram and just check it out to get a snapshot of who you are. And not even just brands, but potential customers ... I'm always like wait what's her Instagram so I can see" (Erin On Demand, 6 January 2026).

**But the credibility argument has a limit nobody mentions, and it is the most useful thing this study found about it.** Half these platforms show a logged-out visitor nothing. I checked all of them on the same day. Instagram, YouTube, Etsy and Snapchat show content to a stranger. Pinterest redirects to an empty shell. Threads returns a login wall. X throws a hard sign-in modal. LinkedIn goes straight to a sign-in page.

**Claiming a handle on X, LinkedIn or Threads buys no credibility whatsoever, because the person checking cannot see it.** That collapses the "be everywhere so you look real" argument for precisely the platforms it is normally used to justify.

Claim the handles anyway. It costs an hour and it protects the name. But that is a defensive trademark action, not a credibility strategy, and it should not be confused with one.

## The platforms

### Instagram

Half of US adults use Instagram, and the age distribution is the best match in this study for who BRAND.md says the buyer is: 80% of 18 to 29 year olds, 62% of 30 to 49 year olds, falling to 40% and then 19% in the older bands (Pew, June 2025). Women use it more than men, 55% to 44%, which matters because BRAND.md identifies women buying gifts as the Q4 commercial buyer. Meta's advertising tools claim 182 million US users, or 64% of adults, which conflicts with Pew's 50%. The gap is the usual one: ad tools count accounts, surveys count people, and a person with a personal account and a brand account is still one buyer. Pew wins.

A post reaches strangers here, and this is confirmed by Meta's own ranking documentation rather than inferred. The system gathers public content, scores it against a viewer's inferred interests, and ranks it, independent of who follows whom. I saw the practical version of this on TikTok rather than Instagram, but the mechanism is the same and the example is worth carrying through the whole report: an LDS modest-clothing brand with **169 followers** had a post surfacing in a major tag next to accounts many times its size. That is roughly Evan's starting position, and it is the clearest evidence available that the settled interest-media finding is real rather than hopeful.

There is a counterweight worth recording. One practitioner argues Instagram discovery is now limited unless you already have a community, and that Instagram "works better as more of a hub and not necessarily a launchpad" (Erin On Demand, 6 January 2026). That is one voice against a documented mechanism, so it does not overturn anything, but it is a fair description of how the platform has been trending, and Metricool's 2026 study measured Instagram reels reach down 35% and post reach down 31% year over year.

What Instagram cannot do is hold anything. A post lives for days. It is not searchable in any way a buyer would use, so nobody types "Manti Temple" next April and finds Evan's carousel from September. And there is no clickable link in a post or a caption, which means every click to the store costs a profile visit first. For a store, that is the structural weakness, and no amount of good content fixes it.

The Latter-day Saint audience here is large and verified. Instagram's own page title reports **1.1 million reels** on #ldstemple and 266,000 on #latterdaysaint. Reading the tag logged out, the top posts carried real numbers: 229,000 views, 180,000, 74,500, 16,100. Nobody at the top of that tag is selling temple art or temple apparel. They are lifestyle, family, humour and podcast accounts. The category is genuinely open, which supports what BRAND.md already believed.

There is a problem in that sentence, though, and it should not be glossed. The two highest-performing posts I observed were both jokes: 229,000 views for "Pros and Cons of having a girlfriend who goes to the temple with you", and 16,100 for "The secret life of this Mormon wife". BRAND.md rules out jokes and meme humour absolutely. **Evan is entering a tag whose winning move is one he has taken off the table.** That is not fatal, and the 74,500-view post was simply the question "What's your favorite temple?", which breaks no rule and lets the audience say which temples they care about. But it means the ceiling here is lower for this brand than the tag's numbers suggest.

Everything the Posting Rota already plans fits Instagram natively: carousels of Evan's own line art, slow pans over drawings with text, sample photography he took himself. No face, no third-party photograph, no dependence on the unsettled garment decision. Metricool's data says carousels generate roughly nine times more saves than single images, and saves are already the metric the Rota watches. Four to five hours a week all in, including the daily commenting practice. If the account goes quiet for a month nothing is destroyed, because reach is per-post rather than per-account.

### Facebook, and why it is three platforms

Facebook has to be split, because treating it as one thing produces a wrong answer. The Page, the Groups and Marketplace behave so differently that they belong in different tiers.

**The Page.** 71% of US adults use Facebook and 52% use it daily, which is the highest daily figure of anything measured. Its age curve is the opposite of everywhere else: it peaks at 80% among 30 to 49 year olds and still reaches 57% of over-65s, and it is the only platform besides YouTube that a majority of every age group uses. Women 78%, men 63%, and daily use is 60% of women against 44% of men. Read against BRAND.md's four avatars, this covers the two that every other platform misses, which are the gift-buying women and the grandmother buyer, and BRAND.md's own Google Trends work says gift intent is the commercial moment.

The genuinely surprising finding of this study is that Facebook organic reach went *up*. Meta reported that views and time spent watching original reels on Facebook approximately doubled in the second half of 2025 against the same period in 2024. Metricool independently measured Facebook reach up 51%, impressions up 57% and interactions up 56% year over year, calling it one of the strongest rebounds across all platforms. Two independent sources, one of them the company. After a decade of Facebook Page reach being the industry's standing joke, it rose sharply in the same year Instagram and TikTok both fell.

A Page post can carry a clickable link, which is one real advantage over Instagram. It has no search memory. It costs ten to twenty minutes a week if it simply receives what Instagram already produces, uploaded natively rather than pushed through the cross-post toggle. And the honest limitation is that the reach rebound is measured across accounts that already have audiences. A Page from zero still starts at zero.

**The Groups, which are the real finding.** This is the largest concentrated, addressable, verified Latter-day Saint audience found anywhere in this research, and it is not close.

Public groups, verified 3 September 2026: Latter Day Saints Uplifting Quotes at 267,000 members. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints (friends, members) at 162,000, running 90 or more posts a day. LDS Dating Midsingles at 45,000. Latter-day Saints around the World at 44,000. President Dallin H. Oaks and Latter-Day Saints Worldwide at 37,000. Latter-day Saints at 28,000. **LDS temples at 22,000, running 20 or more posts a day. LDS Art at 21,000. Latter-Day Saint Temples at 10,000, running 6 posts a day.**

Two temple-specific rooms and one art room. That is not a general audience containing some Latter-day Saints. It is the audience, already sorted by subject, standing in one place with the door open.

There is no algorithm problem here. Membership is the distribution. The variable that decides whether a post is seen is posting velocity: in the 162,000-member group a post competes with 90 others a day and is gone within the hour, while in the 10,000-member temple group at 6 posts a day a single post sits visibly on the page. **Small and slow beats large and fast**, which inverts the instinct to chase the biggest room.

What makes this fit is that Evan's actual asset is a source-verified research library on 119 temples. In a room of 22,000 people who joined because they care about temples, a well-sourced fact about a specific building is not adapted marketing content. It is the native content of that room.

What makes it hard is everything else. It cannot be automated, batched or splintered, so none of the pillar-and-splinter economics that make the rest of the plan cheap apply here. Self-promotion is the fastest route to removal, and admins can decline posts by link. It builds no owned asset, because the group belongs to its moderators. It is two to four hours a week of genuine human attention. And it is the highest-reputation-risk surface in the study, because a misjudged post is seen by a named, gathered community that talks to itself, in a business whose stated growth engine is word of mouth.

One more thing, and it is the reason this section changes a decision rather than just adding a channel. Group posts come from a personal profile, not a Page. A post in "LDS temples" carries a real person's name and photograph to 22,000 people. BRAND.md section 19 lists voice and attribution once Bailee runs social as unresolved, and says it should be settled before the first post. **The moment Facebook Groups enters the plan, that stops being a preference and becomes a fact the audience can see.**

**Marketplace, which is disqualified by a mechanism rather than a judgement.** Meta requires that shipped items use pre-paid Meta-issued labels, that sellers provide tracking and ship within 7 calendar days or have the order auto-cancelled, and that when using a shipping label the seller gets the product to the carrier within 3 business days. Peculiar People is print on demand. Evan never touches the goods and cannot hand a package to a carrier in three days, because the item does not exist until a third party prints and ships it. Meta does not ban print on demand or dropshipping anywhere in its commerce policies; I checked. The fulfilment terms simply assume a seller with stock in a cupboard. Marketplace also sends no traffic to the store, is local by design, and is a price-led surface, which is the wrong environment for a deliberately premium price.

### Pinterest

Pinterest has the best structural fit of any platform in this study and the worst evidence behind it, and both halves of that need saying.

The structural case is strong enough that it almost writes itself. **Pinterest is a search engine, not a feed**, so a pin is found because somebody searched for a thing rather than because they follow you. Followers are close to irrelevant, which is precisely the condition this brand is in. Pinterest states in its own documentation that "there's no set engagement window for Pins, so it's possible that a Pin can gain engagement hours, days, months, or even years after it's first published". **Every pin carries a link, and sending someone to a URL is the intended native behaviour of the format**, which is true of nothing else here. The US and Canada audience is 71.4% female, the strongest female skew in the study, mapping directly onto the gift-buyer avatar. And a catalogue of 119 temples, each with keyword-shaped verified facts already written, is close to ideal raw material.

It is also the cheapest platform here. Roughly thirty minutes a week, generated from assets that already exist, and going dormant for a month costs almost nothing because pins keep working. The Playbook's instruction to start it now because it is slow and then stop thinking about it is exactly right.

Now the problems.

**Pew stopped asking about Pinterest after 2024.** The best survey figure for US usage is 36% of adults, fielded through 10 June 2024, more than two years stale. Age composition is UNKNOWN. Pinterest said on its Q2 2026 earnings call that Gen Z is over half the user base, but that is a company statement made on a call and it is not in the filing.

The company's own filed numbers show a shape worth understanding. Global monthly active users were 640 million for the quarter ended 30 June 2026, up 11%. But US and Canada was 106 million, growing only 4%, while rest of world was 377 million growing 15%. Meanwhile US and Canada revenue per user is $8.30 against $0.23 in the rest of the world. **Pinterest's growth is happening almost entirely in markets a US premium apparel brand cannot sell to.**

And I could not verify the audience at all. Pinterest gates search to logged-out visitors: the search URL redirected to an empty shell and the public topic page returned nothing. **This is the largest single hole in the research, and it sits on the platform with the best structural fit.** Evan can close it in twenty minutes, because the free Pinterest business account the Playbook already put in week one unlocks the keyword data that answers it.

The other honest limitation is that Pinterest cannot do the credibility job. Nobody gets to know a brand on Pinterest, and a logged-out stranger checking whether the brand is real cannot see it. It is a slow, mechanical traffic asset, and it will not produce the first customer.

### TikTok

TikTok is the platform where the settled interest-media finding is most demonstrably true and where the brand is least able to use it.

37% of US adults use TikTok, up from 33% a year earlier and 21% in 2021, and 24% of all US adults use it daily. The age curve is steep: 63% of 18 to 29 year olds against 12% of over-65s. In the US, women use it more than men, 42% to 30%, which is worth noting because TikTok's global ad data shows the opposite and the two get confused.

The interest-media evidence here is the best in the whole study and it is first-hand. **@shopzmci, an LDS modest clothing and temple dress brand with 169 followers, had a post surfacing in the #ldstemple tag** alongside far larger accounts. That is the closest observed analogue to a zero-audience brand getting distribution, and it is worth more than any general claim about how feeds work.

Two things then cut against it hard.

The first is that reach is collapsing. Metricool measured 2.3 million TikTok posts across 92,000 accounts and found views down 31.3%, reach down 28.7% and interactions down 31.2% year over year, while the number of videos published rose 72.1% and image posts rose nearly 140%. Roughly twice the supply chasing two thirds of the attention.

The second is brand safety, and this is the finding I did not expect. Reading #ldstemple logged out, roughly half the tag is ex-Mormon and critical content sitting in the same recommendation pool as the faithful content. Accounts posting under that tag on the day I looked included one explaining that after going through the temple things get "super weird" with the hashtags #exmormon and #cult, one asking "Who are these people and what are they doing? #cult #highcontrolreligion", one explaining temple garments to outsiders, and one tagging a temple wedding post #unworthy. **Instagram's version of the identical tag, checked the same day with the same query, had none of this.**

That matters more here than it would for most brands. Peculiar People is unaffiliated and must never imply affiliation, and BRAND.md rules out politics and combativeness absolutely. Posting reverent temple content into a tag where the adjacent video argues the Church is a high-control religion puts the brand into a conversation it has explicitly decided not to enter, in front of an audience partly there to argue. The promise to answer every comment personally would be a materially different job on TikTok than on Instagram.

On the open TikTok Shop question, I can resolve half of it. **The category is permitted. CONFIRMED.** I read both governing policies directly, each effective 1 September 2026. The US Prohibited Products Policy does not list religious items, and its only religion-adjacent clause bars products promoting discrimination on religion, which is the opposite of what Evan sells. The US Restricted Products Policy does not mention religious items, religious imagery, faith-based products or print-on-demand apparel at all. This corrects a claim in circulation that religious products are barred; a UK and EU version of the policy does restrict "religious or political-related elements", but that is not the US policy.

**Whether a buyer base exists for Latter-day Saint temple art on TikTok Shop specifically remains UNKNOWN, and I do not believe it is knowable from public sources.** TikTok publishes no category sales data. The best available proxy is on Etsy and it is discussed below. What can be added is that the creator-commission mechanism the Playbook admired in Comfrt and Bloom is no longer TikTok-only: Facebook has rolled out affiliate tools where creators self-serve products from a brand catalogue and attach affiliate links to reels, described by practitioners as a direct play for TikTok's marketplace.

TikTok also demands what this brand cannot supply. It rewards personality on camera and near-daily posting, and BRAND.md is explicit that neither operator should be assumed willing to appear. It has no link in a post. And Evan's strongest asset is a written research library, which is the wrong shape for a video-completion machine.

### YouTube, split in two

YouTube is two products and they give opposite answers.

**Long form** is the most attractive platform in this study on paper and the least available in practice. 84% of US adults use YouTube, the highest of anything measured, including 64% of over-65s, and it is now television: Nielsen recorded YouTube as the largest single distributor of US TV viewing for three consecutive months in 2026, reaching 13.8% of all TV watch time in May 2026. Its content lives for years and it is searchable through both YouTube and Google, which makes it one of only three surfaces here with genuine search memory. Metricool found YouTube views per video up 30% while posting rose 25%, the only platform in that study where views grew faster than supply.

And the raw material is perfect. 119 temples of source-verified research, original line art, and a documented body of knowledge about which official sources serve the wrong building. Nobody owns that.

Three things close the door. It is the most expensive content here, six to ten hours a week for one video, and it does not splinter from the two production sessions the Posting Rota is built on. It requires a voice and probably a face, both unresolved. And **the two most natural long-form formats are banned**: BRAND.md forbids showing how the line art is made and forbids filming reference gathering, which removes process and research content entirely.

There is also a competitive problem no other platform has. The Church's own channels own this search space. "Why Mormons Build Temples" has 1,179,426 views. "Salt Lake Temple Time Capsule Opened" has 741,430. "The Process of Building a Latter-day Saint Temple" has 578,049. An unaffiliated brand would be competing for temple search terms against the institution it is required to keep distance from.

Two smaller findings from the same search are useful. The Temple Study format already works here and is already occupied: "Messages of Christ" runs per-temple architectural detail videos at 29,752 and 52,321 views, and "The Covenant Path" runs temple facts and symbolism at 74,564. Meanwhile temple art is a dead zone. Searching for LDS temple line art and drawing returns a ceiling of 11,702 views, then 826, 459, 326, 44 and 32. LDS apparel is the same story, topping out at 3,170. Nobody makes art-process content about temples, and when they do, nobody watches. Which is convenient, because Evan is barred from making it anyway.

**Shorts** inherits none of what makes YouTube good. Days of shelf life, no search compounding, no carousels so three of the Rota's five weekly slots cannot go there at all, and a monetisation door that is effectively shut: the affiliate programme needs Partner Programme membership, which needs either 1,000 subscribers with 4,000 watch hours or 1,000 subscribers with 10 million Shorts views in 90 days, and from 1 February 2027 those thresholds roughly double for new applicants.

The one genuinely useful thing about Shorts is a format finding. The six-month cross-posting test found that Instagram-native trend content dies on Shorts, and that Shorts viewers want explanatory, tutorial-style content instead. **That means Temple Study is a better fit for Shorts than The Inspection is, which is the opposite of how the two shows split on Instagram.** Ten minutes a week to receive the vertical video that already exists.

### Snapchat

Snapchat had never been assessed for this brand. The question was whether it does anything the others do not. The answer is no, and the company's own filing makes the case more efficiently than any argument could.

For the quarter ended 30 June 2026, Snap reported 493 million daily active users globally, up 5%. **North America was 92 million, down 7% year over year and flat against the previous quarter.** Europe was down 2%. Rest of world was up 12%. Snap is growing by growing everywhere except the market Peculiar People sells to. Every other platform in this study is flat or growing in the US.

The demographics finish it. 25% of US adults use Snapchat, and it is the only platform in the study whose usage *fell* in Pew's tracking, from 27% in 2023 and 2024 to 25% in 2025. By age: 58% of 18 to 29 year olds, then 31%, then **13% of 50 to 64 year olds and 4% of over-65s**. Two of BRAND.md's four avatars are effectively absent.

The reconnaissance was worse than the demographics. Snapchat is one of the few platforms that shows a logged-out visitor real data, so I could check properly. **Searching "lds temple" returned Hindu and other temples, an account called "Rejoice Temple", one called "Dr Bryan Spell Temple" advertising "Restore Your Broken Homes | Manifestations", and "Mr Mateta Temple".** The single most important search term for this brand returns noise.

Searching "lds" and "latter day saint" found the community does exist. Spotlight videos ran 3,500 to 86,000 views on lifestyle, apologetics and explainer content. But the dedicated accounts had 3, 6, 17, 432 and 832 followers, and every result above 8,000 followers turned out to be a false match, including a French motorcyclist and a French optician. The view counts run roughly an order of magnitude below what the same community produces on Instagram, where I observed 74,000 to 229,000 on the same day.

Add zero shelf life, no commerce mechanism worth using, and a tone that is casual, ephemeral and face-led against a brand that is premium, reverent and understated, and there is nothing here. The one thing Snapchat is genuinely distinctive at, ephemeral private sharing between close friends, is a property a brand cannot use.

### X, Threads, LinkedIn and Reddit

These four fail for different reasons but they share one: I could not see any of them logged out, so none of them can do the credibility job either.

**X** reaches 21% of US adults and only 10% daily, the lowest daily figure measured. It is the most male platform here, 25% of men against 16% of women by survey and 65.6% male by ad data, which fights the female gift-buyer avatar directly. Its ranking code is public and does include link penalty modelling, though the magnitude is UNKNOWN. Shelf life is hours. And the deeper problem is temperamental rather than technical: X rewards taking positions, and BRAND.md rules out both politics and jokes absolutely, which removes the two engines of X reach. A brand that must never imply Church affiliation while never taking a position is unusually exposed on the platform built for taking positions.

**Threads** is the smallest platform profiled, at 8% of US adults. That figure is one of the most reliable numbers in the study, because Pew's 8% and Meta's ad-tool 8.9% agree within a point, which almost nothing else does. Threads is genuinely easy for a zero-follower account to be seen on, and Evan's written, factual material is a natural fit for a text platform in a way it is not for TikTok. But it is small, shelf life is hours, there is no commerce, Google indexing of Threads posts is UNKNOWN despite a lot of confident claims about it, and it is gated to logged-out visitors.

**LinkedIn** has the thinnest evidence base in the study. Pew stopped asking about it after 2024, when 32% of US adults used it. Microsoft reports LinkedIn revenue but no member count. DataReportal's ad-tool figure of 270 million US registered members, rendered as 98.3% of US adults, fails a basic sanity check and was discarded. Distribution is follower-driven rather than interest-driven, so a zero-audience account genuinely starts at zero here, which is the exact problem interest media was supposed to solve. Metricool measured impressions down 23% and interactions down 14%. And the fundamental problem is context: people are on LinkedIn in a professional frame, and a reverent, personal, faith-linked purchase is not a professional-frame purchase. Evan could write genuinely good LinkedIn content about automation and fact verification. It would reach people interested in ecommerce operations, not a woman in Idaho buying her son a Manti Temple sweatshirt.

**Reddit** is the one I could not check at all, and that has to be said plainly rather than papered over. reddit.com is blocked to both my tools by policy, so I could not open a single subreddit or count a single subscriber.

What the filings show is genuinely interesting though. For the quarter ended 30 June 2026, Reddit reported 53.2 million US daily uniques and 197.2 million US weekly uniques. And of 130.3 million global daily uniques, **52.6 million were logged in and 77.7 million were logged out**. Sixty percent of Reddit's daily audience is not signed in, which is a search-arrival pattern rather than a social one. People land from Google, read one thread, and leave. That makes Reddit's real property durable Google-indexed shelf life, which is worth more than its social surface.

Against that: Reddit punishes commercial intent harder than anywhere else. A practitioner describes it as "its own kind of immune system", where engaged users call out bad-faith content and moderators police it with automations, and where "if you put a post on Reddit specifically to get something out of it, it will get downvoted to heck" (Noah Hallett, 12 February 2026, 229 views, so a single low-reach source). Users publicly audit account age and karma. The recommended legitimate play for a small operator is to create your own company subreddit rather than embedding promotion in other people's, which is honest but slow, and which carries a warning worth quoting given what this study found on TikTok: an official subreddit concentrates criticism, and "if people want to say really snarky, nasty things about your company, you have to either welcome that in or they're going to say it elsewhere".

### Etsy, which was added

Etsy is not a social platform and does not belong in a tier list of feeds. It earns a place because it answered a question four rounds of research could not, for free, in fifteen minutes.

**It is the only place where this exact product already sells in public with prices visible to anyone.** Observed on 3 September 2026, logged out, the LDS temple shirt listings ran: $7.42 for a personalised custom design, $13.20 for "I Love to See the Temple Tee, LDS Temple Illustration", $14.99 for Salt Lake Temple Open House family shirts, $18.99, $19.15, $21.74, $22.99, $23.99, $24.98 for a Taylorsville Utah Temple shirt, $26.00 and $26.52.

**The observed band is roughly $13 to $27, heavily discounted.** Peculiar People's tee is $44.99 and its hoodie $74.99, which is 1.7 to 3.5 times that band. BRAND.md records Evan holding a premium price with a stated worry that it is a barrier while the store has no reviews. This is the first hard evidence of what the comparison actually looks like, and the Playbook's Comfrt story, where a brand launched too high and did $10,000 on a day it projected $100,000 before cutting to $69, reads differently next to it.

But there is a premium counter-example and it is important. PaperTempleArt of Dallas, Texas has been on Etsy 16 years, has 113 sales and a 5.0 rating from 28 reviews, and was active on 1 September 2026. It sells per-temple paper art shadow boxes at **$80.00**, a custom "any temple" version at **$150.00** marked "Only 2 left", a temple night light at $45.00, and a "Bulk Temple Ornament, Christmas Gift for Wards and Relief Society" at $50.00.

**Premium, per-temple, art-led temple products do sustain $80 to $150. What they do not demonstrate is volume: 113 sales in 16 years.** Both halves matter. The price ceiling is higher than the shirt band suggests. The demand depth is the open question, and it is the honest answer to whether a buyer base exists for temple art at a premium: yes, but thin.

Two further observations. The shirt sellers are mostly not temple brands: AllegrowDesigns, with 168 sales over four years, sells mainly viola and orchestra joke shirts, and the LDS temple shirt is one item in a generic spread across unrelated niches. That supports BRAND.md's claim that nobody is doing this well. And the bulk ward and Relief Society product independently corroborates the group-order play the Playbook calls the fastest realistic route to a first order from a stranger.

As a channel, Etsy should not be used. A 25.9% take rate against print-on-demand cost of goods breaks the margin, and the marketplace owns the customer, the email address and the review, which is incompatible with BRAND.md's vision of a known brand. Use it as evidence, not as a shop.

## The recommendation

### Primary: two platforms, and they do different jobs

**Instagram.** This is the publishing platform. It has a verified audience of the right age and sex, it reaches strangers without a follower base, and every format the Posting Rota already plans fits it natively using nothing but material Evan owns. It is also where a buyer who looks the brand up will actually look, so it carries the credibility job as well as the reach job. Nothing here changes the Posting Rota's existing plan; it confirms it.

**Facebook Groups.** This is the distribution platform, and it is the addition this research argues for. 22,000 people in "LDS temples", 10,000 in "Latter-Day Saint Temples", 21,000 in "LDS Art", plus a further quarter of a million across the general Latter-day Saint groups. No algorithm stands between a post and that audience. The research library is native content there rather than adapted content. Target the slow rooms rather than the big ones: the 10,000-member temple group at six posts a day gives a single post more visibility than the 162,000-member group at ninety posts a day.

**Two conditions on Facebook Groups, and they are real conditions rather than caveats.** First, it needs the second operator, because it is pure human attention and cannot be batched or automated. Second, **the attribution question in BRAND.md section 19 has to be answered before the first group post**, because group posting carries a real name and photograph to 22,000 people and there is no brand-neutral way to do it.

### Secondary: receive the asset, spend minutes not hours

These four get the content Instagram already produced, uploaded natively, and nothing else. Combined cost is under an hour a week.

**Facebook Page.** Ten to twenty minutes. Reach rose 51% year over year while everything else fell, its demographics cover the gift-buying and grandmother avatars better than anything else, and it carries clickable links. Never use the Instagram cross-post toggle; upload the clean file natively.

**Pinterest.** Thirty minutes, generated from assets that already exist. It is the only platform here with years of shelf life, real searchability and unthrottled links all at once, and its 71% female audience matches the gift buyer. Start it now because it is slow, then stop thinking about it, exactly as the Playbook says. **Before committing anything to it, spend twenty minutes behind the free business account checking whether the audience is there**, because I could not.

**TikTok.** Ten to fifteen minutes, receive-only, no separate production. One specific instruction that comes out of this research: **do not lead with #ldstemple on TikTok.** Roughly half that tag is ex-Mormon and "cult" framing content, and the brand does not belong in that argument. Use temple-name, architecture and location tags instead. Revisit TikTok properly only if the brand ever decides someone will appear on camera.

**YouTube Shorts.** Ten minutes, receive-only. Note the format inversion: Temple Study fits Shorts better than The Inspection does, which is the reverse of how they split on Instagram.

### Presence only: claim the handle, post rarely or never

**Threads, X, LinkedIn, and a YouTube channel.** Claim all four handles to protect the name. That is a defensive action costing about an hour, and it should not be confused with a credibility strategy, because Threads, X and LinkedIn all show a logged-out stranger a login wall. Nobody checking whether this brand is real can see them.

The YouTube channel is worth claiming for a different reason: it holds the Shorts, and it is the place a long-form library would eventually live if the voice and face questions ever resolve.

### Skip

**Snapchat.** Assessed for the first time and it should be the last time unless something changes. Its US audience shrank 7% year over year, it misses two of the four avatars on age alone, it has no shelf life, and its search returns Hindu temples for the brand's core term.

**Facebook Marketplace.** Disqualified by mechanism. Meta requires a package handed to a carrier within three business days on a Meta-issued label, and a print-on-demand seller cannot do that.

**Etsy as a sales channel.** The 25.9% take rate breaks a print-on-demand margin and the marketplace owns the customer. Use it once as free market research and do not sell there.

**Reddit, deferred rather than skipped.** I could not check it, so I will not rule on it. Reddit has real properties this brand could use: years of Google-indexed shelf life and a community that values exactly the verified-research contribution Evan can make. It also punishes commercial intent harder than anywhere else and requires months of account history first. **Someone should spend five minutes opening the relevant subreddits and reading the subscriber counts before this is decided**, and that someone should not be me guessing.

### One thing that is not a social platform and matters more than most of them

**Publish the temple facts as indexed pages on peculiarpeopleco.com.** The research is written, verified and sitting inside product descriptions where it does a fraction of the work it could. As indexed pages it becomes a durable, owned answer to "when was the Manti Temple dedicated" that keeps working for years and sends traffic to a store the brand controls.

Only three surfaces in this entire study have durable search memory (Pinterest, YouTube long form and Reddit), and all three hand the compounding to somebody else. A blog is the same property, owned. It is slow, it will not produce a first customer this quarter, and none of that is a reason not to start it with material that already exists.

### What is lost by not being in the primary tier

- **Facebook Page:** the clickable link, and the only measured reach increase in the study. Mitigated because it receives the content anyway.
- **Pinterest:** compounding search traffic, which is the only thing here that grows without more work. This is the largest real loss, and it is mitigated by the fact that it still runs at thirty minutes a week.
- **TikTok:** the platform with the best demonstrated ability to give a 169-follower brand distribution. Genuinely lost, and accepted because of the tag environment and the on-camera requirement.
- **YouTube Shorts:** almost nothing. Shorts has no property that Instagram Reels lacks.
- **YouTube long form:** the strongest long-term asset in the study. Lost for now because two of its natural formats are banned and it needs a third production pillar the plan does not have.
- **Threads:** the lowest-friction reach for a zero-follower account, on the smallest audience in the study. Little lost.
- **X:** nothing. Wrong sex skew, wrong temperament, hours of shelf life, penalised links.
- **LinkedIn:** an audience of ecommerce operators, which is not the buyer.
- **Snapchat:** nothing verified.
- **Reddit:** durable Google-indexed shelf life and a community that rewards verified research. Unquantified because unverified.
- **Facebook Marketplace:** nothing. It cannot send traffic and cannot be fulfilled.
- **Etsy:** a working search-driven sales channel, at the cost of the margin and the customer relationship.

## The case against this recommendation

Written as strongly as I can make it, because a recommendation that has not been attacked properly is not worth acting on.

**It puts primary weight on the most fragile thing in the study.** Facebook Groups is unautomatable, unmeasurable, dependent on a second operator whose role BRAND.md explicitly says is unconfirmed, and it forces an unresolved attribution decision before a single post. It is the least like the rest of the plan and the most likely to stop. Everything else in the Posting Rota was designed so that one person could do it while the kettle boils. This adds a channel that only works if a specific other person shows up every week and is willing to have their name and face on it.

**It demotes the platform with the single best piece of evidence in the whole report.** The most valuable observation here is a 169-follower brand account getting distribution in a major tag. That happened on TikTok. TikTok is then pushed to secondary on the strength of one tag read on one day, and the safety concern, while real, is a judgement about adjacency rather than a measured harm. Nobody has shown that a reverent temple post in a mixed tag damages a brand. The report may have talked itself out of the best available distribution on a hunch.

**It bets primary effort on a platform where the winning move is banned.** The report says so itself: the two highest-performing posts observed in #ldstemple on Instagram were both jokes, and BRAND.md rules out jokes absolutely. So the recommendation is to concentrate on the platform where the brand cannot use the format that wins, while its reach fell 31% to 35% year over year. That is a strange place to put the primary effort.

**It demotes Pinterest on ignorance rather than evidence.** Pinterest has years of shelf life, real searchability, unthrottled links, a 71% female audience matching the Q4 gift buyer, and the lowest cost per week of anything here. It is the best structural fit in the study and it is placed second because I could not see it. Demoting the best-fitting platform because of a gap in the research is a research failure being converted into a business decision.

**The concentration argument rests on opinion while the breadth argument rests on measurement.** Every source arguing for focus is a practitioner with a view: a YouTube video, a critique, another YouTube video. The one thing that was actually tested, over six months, is that cross-posting carries no penalty. So the measured evidence supports breadth and the unmeasured evidence supports focus, and the report went with the unmeasured evidence.

**And the deepest objection: with zero orders, none of this analysis has any feedback in it.** Every tier here is assigned on demographics, observation and mechanics. Not one placement is based on evidence about who actually buys Latter-day Saint temple apparel, because no one ever has. A completely different and defensible strategy is available: post the same five assets to all eight viable surfaces for ninety days at a marginal cost of under two hours a week total, and let sales rather than analysis decide the tiers. That answers the question with data instead of with reasoning, and this brand has no data.

### Does it survive?

**Mostly, and it needs three amendments.**

The concentration objection attacks a stricter recommendation than the one made. The recommendation is not "be on two platforms". It is **produce for one, distribute to several, and only count the ones you will actually read.** That already is the low-cost breadth position: eight surfaces receive content, and the total secondary cost is under an hour a week. What is concentrated is attention and measurement, not distribution. On that reading the measured evidence and the practitioner evidence agree rather than conflict, and the zero-feedback objection is largely answered too, because the plan does post widely and cheaply. The one thing it refuses to do is spread the *reading* across eight platforms, and that refusal stands: three data points per platform teaches nothing.

The Instagram objection is half right and it changes the expectation rather than the placement. Instagram remains where the verified audience, the demographic match and the credibility function all sit, and no other platform has all three. But the ceiling is genuinely lower for a brand that cannot make jokes, and the plan should expect slower numbers in that tag than the tag's top posts suggest. The 74,500-view "What's your favorite temple?" post is the proof that a non-joke format can work there, and it is a thinner proof than I would like.

**Amendment one: TikTok.** The objection lands. Do not demote it on adjacency alone. Keep it receiving the video at fifteen minutes a week, drop #ldstemple as a tag, and treat the environment as a reason to change tags rather than a reason to retreat. If a post in temple-name and architecture tags performs, that is real information and it costs nothing to find out.

**Amendment two: Pinterest.** The objection lands completely. Its placement is provisional and it is provisional on my failure, not on the platform. **Check the audience behind the free business account before acting on this tier at all.** If the search volume is there, Pinterest moves up, and it moves up cheaply because it costs thirty minutes a week either way.

**Amendment three: Facebook Groups.** The fragility objection lands. It should be stated as conditional rather than primary: it is the largest verified audience in the study, and it is worth the primary slot only if the second operator is confirmed and the attribution decision is made first. If either is unresolved, it drops to secondary and Instagram carries the plan alone, which is what the Posting Rota already assumed.

## Where this collides with what is already settled

Six things here either contradict or complicate existing positions. None of them overturn a settled finding outright, and I want to be explicit about which is which.

**1. The settled interest-media finding survives, with one qualification.** Direct first-hand confirmation: a 169-follower brand account surfaced in a major tag. Against it, one practitioner argues Instagram discovery is now limited without an existing community and that Instagram is "a hub, not a launchpad". That is one voice against Meta's own documented mechanism, so it qualifies the finding rather than overturning it. Expect interest media to work, and expect it to work less well on Instagram in 2026 than the phrase implies.

**2. "No one else is doing this, and certainly no one is doing it well" needs splitting.** The product claim holds: nobody is selling original per-temple line art apparel, and the Etsy supply is mostly generic sellers spraying niches. **The content claim does not.** Temple Study is already running in at least three places: "Messages of Christ" on YouTube at 29,752 and 52,321 views per temple, "The Covenant Path" at 74,564, and "Tia Smith" posting "Temple Tip" videos on TikTok about temple symbolism "most members never notice". The format is proven, which is good news, and it is occupied, which is not what BRAND.md currently assumes.

**3. The premium price now has a number against it.** BRAND.md records Evan holding a premium price with a stated worry that it is a barrier. The observed Etsy band for LDS temple tees is $13 to $27 against $44.99. That is a real collision. It is softened, genuinely, by PaperTempleArt sustaining $80 to $150 for per-temple art, which says the ceiling is set by what the thing is rather than by the category. But 113 sales in 16 years says the volume at that ceiling is thin. Both facts belong in the pricing conversation and neither is currently in BRAND.md.

**4. The Posting Rota's open question about Facebook is answered, and the answer changes the plan.** The Rota says "ask yourself whether a page or a group serves you better ... that is worth an hour of your own reconnaissance." I did the hour. **Groups, decisively**, by roughly an order of magnitude of concentrated audience. Neither operating document currently treats Groups as a channel; the Playbook mentions group moderators only as seeding targets. This research promotes Groups from a seeding tactic to a primary distribution channel, which is the single biggest change proposed here.

**5. The TikTok Shop unknown is half resolved, and half of it is not resolvable.** The category is permitted, CONFIRMED from both governing US policies effective 1 September 2026, which corrects a claim in circulation that religious products are barred. Whether a buyer base exists on TikTok Shop for this specifically remains UNKNOWN and I do not believe public sources can answer it. Two things do change the shape of the question: Etsy shows premium temple art sells at $80 to $150 in small volumes, and Facebook has now built its own creator-commission affiliate mechanism, so that mechanism is no longer a reason to be on TikTok specifically.

**6. Two findings that are not in any document and should be checked.** Multiple Etsy sellers are already merchandising a **Salt Lake Temple Open House in 2027**, and an Instagram post at 180,000 views states "Next year there will be 6 months of public tours". Separately, the "Rejoice in Christ" 2027 youth theme is already on shirts. Both are SINGLE SOURCE and neither is verified against a Church source. If the open house is real, it is the largest occasion-driven demand event available in the planning window and it lands on the brand's most obvious temple. That deserves a five-minute check against churchofjesuschristtemples.org before it goes anywhere near a plan.

**One thing that does not collide.** Nothing found contradicts the 98% no-CTA rule, the five-then-nine cadence, the two-show structure, the Pinterest ads rejection, or the constraint that only material Evan owns outright may be published. All of those held up.

## What I could not find out

Listed plainly, because these are the places where a confident-sounding sentence would have been the wrong output.

- **Whether a Latter-day Saint audience exists on Pinterest.** Gated logged out. The largest gap in the research, on the best-fitting platform. Twenty minutes behind a free business account closes it.
- **Whether one exists on Reddit, Threads, X or LinkedIn.** Login walls on three; Reddit blocked to my tools entirely.
- **Whether there is a TikTok Shop buyer base for this category.** Not knowable from public sources.
- **Whether Meta's "original content" rule quietly means "original to Facebook".** Marketers raised it, could not resolve it, and neither could I. It is the most consequential open question for the cross-posting plan.
- **Whether Pinterest or any social channel converts better than another for a store like this.** Every figure in circulation traces to content farms with no primary data. Salesforce measures social in aggregate and only says traffic is up 29% while orders are up 1%.
- **Current Pinterest and LinkedIn demographics.** Pew stopped asking after 2024.
- **The magnitude of X's link penalty.** Its existence is visible in the published ranking code; the size is not.
- **Shorts-specific audience data.** Google publishes none.
