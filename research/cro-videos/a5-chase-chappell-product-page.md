# A5. How To Increase Conversion Rates on Shopify (2026 Guide)

## Source
- Channel: Chase Chappell
- Length 32:56 total, published 5 Dec 2025, ~27k views
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=daDsoHXE52A
- Watched: 08:50 to 18:03 only, the chapter "How To Setup A Product Page on Shopify".
  Balanced detail, 45 scene-selected frames, native captions. Two numbers were re-extracted
  at full resolution and cropped to read them reliably.
- Skipped deliberately: site speed, backend metrics, cart recovery email, subscriptions.
  None apply to a store with no traffic and no orders.

Screen recording of three live stores: Graza (olive oil), Everyday Dose (coffee), Carpe
(deodorant).

## Testable claims

1. The first thing the page shows matches the thing that was clicked to get there. He calls
   this the "first load experience": if the ad showed the packaging, the top of the landing
   page shows that same packaging.
2. Product images show the item in use in its real setting, not only the object. On Graza
   he points to images of the oil being poured, used in baking, and sitting in a kitchen.
3. Variant, quantity and purchase-frequency choices sit in one view, so no page reload is
   needed to configure the order. He contrasts this with a site where options are stacked
   across separate loads.
4. Quantity tiers are shown as selectable tiles with labels on them. On Graza these carry
   "Most Popular" and "Best Value" badges.
5. The cart is a drawer that shows the items without leaving the page.
6. Below the fold, the page answers the practical questions in named blocks. On Graza:
   how big the bottle is, how it is harvested, how to use it, refill options, ingredients,
   where it is grown, how it is made, and recipes.
7. His stated reason for the recipes block is objection handling: "if people don't know
   what they should do with this product, well, those are objections."
8. Third-party credibility is cited by name rather than asserted. Everyday Dose names
   Tasting Table and Who What Wear.
9. The page answers sensory questions explicitly. For coffee: what does it taste like, will
   I feel jittery, is it drinkable.
10. Customer proof shows faces of people using the product, not only star ratings and text.
11. A comparison block states the difference against the alternative the buyer is currently
    using. Everyday Dose runs a table against regular coffee.
12. A before-and-after is shown where the product's effect is visible. Carpe does this.
13. Benefit callouts are short scannable lines, not paragraphs.
14. The cart button says "Continue" rather than "Checkout" when more offers follow.

## Apparel-specific

None. Three consumables and a deodorant. Claims 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 9 and 13 transfer to
apparel; the rest are built on repeat-purchase economics a temple tee does not have.

## What was shown that contradicts what was said

**The orders number is wrong.** At 13:45 he says "Over 6 million orders delivered? Well,
why wouldn't you buy this product if 6 million other people have?" The Everyday Dose page
on screen at that moment reads **"5,000,000+ Orders"**. I cropped and re-read the frame at
full resolution to be sure. He inflated the brand's own claim by a million while arguing
that big numbers persuade. Small thing, but it is the exact failure mode this research is
supposed to guard against, and it is a reason to treat every number he speaks as unchecked.

For fairness, one number I initially thought was wrong is not: he says 86% off and the page
does read "86% OFF TODAY" against $25 from $179. That checked out.

**"Masterclass in conversion rates."** He calls the Graza page this, and separately says
Carpe is "on pace to become a billion-dollar company." No evidence for either is shown. No
analytics, no test, no source. These are assertions about businesses he does not appear to
work with.

**The whole chapter is an argument for tools.** It ends by recommending a free audit
builder from Sirge, which is an affiliate link in the description, and the AOV section
immediately after resolves to another paid app. The observations about what is on the
Graza and Everyday Dose pages are real and visible. The causal claims about why those pages
convert are not evidenced.

**Everyday Dose is not a model this brand can copy.** The page he praises most is built on
86% off, a strike-through from $179 to $25, a prize draw ("a Chance To Be 1 of 30 Cash
Winners"), and a "Clinicians' Choice" marque. That is a discount-led acquisition funnel.
It is the opposite of a premium brand holding its price, which is what BRAND.md commits to.

## Statistics stated (single source, and one of them misquoted)

- "Over 6 million orders" for Everyday Dose. The page says 5,000,000+. Discard.
- 93.6% say they love the taste, 88% feel a positive impact. These are Everyday Dose's own
  on-page marketing claims, repeated by the presenter. Not independent evidence of anything.
- Over 10,000 research publications support the ingredients. Same, the brand's own claim.

Nothing from this source survives the two-source rule.

## Relevance to Peculiar People

- Claim 3 is the strongest takeaway and it corroborates A2 independently: get the whole
  configuration into one view. For a store where a buyer picks temple, garment, colour,
  size and possibly a date, this is the central design problem of the product page.
- Claim 6 is worth noting because Peculiar People already does a version of it. The temple
  facts section is exactly this kind of below-the-fold answer block, and BRAND.md records
  that it was collapsed into rows because it had grown to about six phone screens. Two
  sources now argue that block earns its place; the open question is only its presentation.
- Claim 1 has no application yet. There are no ads and no traffic.
- Claims 4, 11, 12, 14 are built on discounting, comparison against competitors, and
  post-purchase upsell stacking. All three sit badly with the reverent, premium positioning,
  and the discount mechanics also collide with print-on-demand margin.
- Claim 10 is blocked. No customer faces exist.
- Overall this is the weakest of the five general sources. It stays in the record because
  claims 2, 3, 6 and 9 are independently corroborated elsewhere, but nothing unique to it
  should carry weight on its own.
