# B1. Optimizing Conversion Rates For A Clothing Ecommerce Brand (Optimization By Oliver, Ep 62)

## Source
- Channel: Oliver Kenyon, ConversionWise. Presenter is Oliver Kenyon, co-founder.
- Length 6:57, published 5 Feb 2024
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jhr68ZeushM
- Watched: whole video, balanced detail, 50 scene-selected frames, native captions.

A before-and-after redesign of Bonobos, a menswear brand, shown on mobile throughout.
Three pages: home, cart drawer, product page. This is the most directly relevant source on
the watchlist: apparel, mobile, and a real trading store.

## Testable claims

Announcement bar and above the fold
1. The announcement bar is one line and one message, with the action word underlined and
   linked. The "before" bar ran to three lines of terms and codes.
2. The space saved by shrinking the bar is spent on content above the fold, not left empty.
3. The homepage hero shows a person wearing the product. His reasoning, stated for apparel
   specifically: "especially in things like men's apparel brands like this, to actually
   showcase and influence people via visual social proof."
4. A star rating and customer count appear above the fold on the homepage.
5. There is exactly one call to action above the fold.
6. On mobile the primary button is full width. He points out that in the "before" the button
   was a small square reading "Shop Sale" and says most viewers could not find it.
7. The button label is motivating rather than transactional, and carries a directional arrow.
   His replacement: "Discover Your Perfect Fit Now →" instead of "Shop Sale".
8. "As seen in" press logos sit above the fold.

Cart drawer
9. The cart shows a free shipping threshold with a visual progress bar.
10. A second threshold above free shipping unlocks a gift, so there is a reason to keep
    adding after shipping is earned.
11. The cart line items are compact, so what matters is not pushed below the fold.
12. Cross-sell items in the cart have a high-contrast button. He criticises the original
    for a white button on a white background: "it's just not going to get clicked."
13. Cross-sells are a carousel with visible arrows so the shopper can browse without
    leaving the cart.
14. A customer review appears inside the cart drawer.
15. The checkout button is reachable without scrolling on mobile.
16. Payment method icons sit under the checkout button.

Product page
17. A trust policy bar sits directly below the navigation, carrying free shipping and
    money-back guarantees. In the after it reads "Free Shipping & Easy Returns".
18. A rating pill is overlaid on the product image itself.
19. Image thumbnails are visible under the gallery. His reasoning: do not assume people know
    they can swipe. "Keep it simple stupid, treat people like they're 5 years old."
20. **Colour options are shown as photographs of the product in that colour, not as plain
    colour dots.** His words: "using colour swatches is fine, but actually using coloured
    pictures of the images themselves... typically drives more revenue... it allows people
    to visualise what colour is actually changing on the product."
21. The size guide link sits above the fold. He says he is "amazed" Bonobos does not have it
    there.
22. The size selector sits above the fold.
23. The same call-to-action wording and styling is used on the homepage, product page and
    cart, so the funnel is visually consistent.

## Apparel-specific, called out by name

Claims 3, 20, 21 and 22 are explicitly apparel reasoning, not general CRO:
- Show the garment worn by a person, because apparel is bought visually.
- Show colour variants as photographs of the garment, because the buyer needs to see what
  the colour does to the product, not what the colour is.
- Put the size guide and the size picker above the fold, because size is the blocking
  question in apparel and everything stalls until it is answered.

That trio is the sharpest apparel-specific finding in the research so far.

## What was shown that contradicts what was said

**The homepage rating is vague where A2 demands precision.** His redesigned homepage reads
"4.9/5 by Thousands of Happy Customers", while his own redesigned product page reads
"4.9/5 by 78 of Happy Customers". A2 argued explicitly that round or vague quantities
("200+") read as estimates or as fake, and that a specific count is more believable. B1 uses
the vague form on the highest-traffic page. Direct conflict between two sources on how to
express social proof. My read for the synthesis: A2 has the better argument, and B1's own
product page, which uses the exact number 78, is the more defensible of his two treatments.

**He is honest about the evidence, which is worth crediting.** He says the cart changes
"would generate these guys 7 to eight figures in additional revenue per year," then
immediately adds "of course it goes without saying, everything has to be tested." He is
describing a proposal, and says so. That is a materially better standard than A2, which
presented an untested redesign as a measured 3.5x result, and better than A5, which called
a page a "masterclass" with no evidence. Nothing here is claimed as a test result.

**It is still a sales pitch.** The video closes by asking for optimisation work, and
Bonobos is not his client. He is auditing a brand he has no data for. Every claim is a
practitioner's opinion, informed by 11 years of doing this, and should be weighted as that
rather than as evidence.

**Free shipping and gift thresholds are shown but not costed.** Claims 9 and 10 assume the
retailer can absorb shipping above a threshold and give away a gift. Nothing in the video
addresses margin.

## Statistics stated

None. He gives no percentages, no benchmarks, and no test results anywhere in the video.
That is unusual for the genre and, given the two-source rule, it means there is nothing here
to discard.

## Relevance to Peculiar People

- Claim 20 is the single most valuable finding for this store. Peculiar People sells one
  design across dozens of colourways on garments where the print is the product, and the
  art renders white on dark colours and black on light ones. A plain colour dot cannot
  convey that. This is an apparel-specific argument for showing each colourway as an image,
  and it corroborates A2's "no dropdowns, use visual swatches" from an apparel direction.
- Claims 21 and 22 are directly checkable and directly relevant. BRAND.md records that the
  measurements table is trimmed at assembly and the size guide is a video. Whether that
  video is reachable above the fold is exactly the kind of thing the phase 2 audit must
  look at rather than assume.
- Claim 3 is the mockup problem again, now stated by an apparel specialist. Third source to
  land on it.
- Claim 17 is cheap and available: a policy bar under the nav needs no assets, only text.
- Claims 9, 10 and 12 to 14 concern the cart drawer. Free shipping thresholds and gifts run
  straight into print-on-demand margin, and the in-cart review needs reviews that do not
  exist. Flag as blocked or conflicted rather than dropping.
- Claim 23 is free and worth checking: does the store use the same button wording and style
  across home, product and cart, or does each page invent its own?
