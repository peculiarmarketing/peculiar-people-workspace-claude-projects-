# C2. 15 Amazing Ecommerce Storefronts (Part 1)

## Source
- Channel: Shogun (a page-builder company)
- Length 10:47, published 9 Jan 2026
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FhwyswSAtls
- Watched: whole video, balanced detail, 50 scene-selected frames, native captions.

Fifteen storefronts, roughly 35 seconds each, each with a named design decision and a
one-line takeaway. Purely positive examples, no criticism anywhere. The four apparel or
apparel-adjacent brands are FRYE, Adanola, Flag & Anthem and BRUNT Workwear; Pepper and
Gathre are also relevant.

## Testable claims

Visual consistency, the most important finding here
1. **Category pages use the same angle, background and lighting across every product.** Said
   of FRYE, with the mechanism named explicitly: "which creates visual continuity, a huge
   trust builder for higher priced items."
2. The line that follows it is the sharpest statement of the whole trust problem found in
   this research: **"Clean visual systems build trust faster than discounts ever could."**

Above the fold on the product page
3. All decision-making information is on the first screen with no scrolling. Said of FRYE,
   listing: sizes, stock status, payment flexibility, and reviews.
4. Said of BRUNT Workwear as a general rule: "put the decision making details above the
   fold."
5. Said of Flag & Anthem: "product pages keep fit details and options high on the page."

Fit, for apparel
6. The hero answers the category's biggest fit question before it is asked. Adanola puts
   leg lengths (7/8, regular, tall) at the top of the homepage, because fit is the biggest
   friction point in leggings.
7. Product pages use tall image stacks and photographs on real bodies, so shoppers can
   "judge fabric stretch and silhouette without zooming around."
8. **Model measurements are published.** Said of Pepper, alongside fit notes and reviews,
   "for a tricky category." Third independent source for this claim, after B2 and B3.
9. Product cards on category pages show reviews and savings.

Photography style
10. Lifestyle photographs show scale and real use rather than over-polished staging. Said of
    Gathre: "soft lifestyle photos that show scale and real life use instead of
    overpolished staging."

Navigation
11. Navigation is organised by how customers use the product, not by what the product is
    called. Stated as the takeaway for Glade, and demonstrated by:
    - Microfiber Wholesale, organised by industry (auto, janitorial, hospitality,
      residential), "mirrors how professionals think";
    - BRUNT, organised by trade;
    - Dylan's Candy Bar, organised by occasion, "which makes finding gifts ridiculously easy";
    - Flag & Anthem, split into men, women, game day, performance.
12. Homepage sections sit where returning customers expect them. Said of Adanola. The
    takeaway given: "design your homepage like a guided conversation, not a gallery of
    images."

Page structure
13. Emotional content first, rational content second. Said of Rumpl: hero images for vibe,
    then icons, specs, warranty and sustainability, then long-form copy. "Lead with emotion,
    close with clarity."
14. Detail sections sit in accordions so the page can be skimmed. Said of Balance of Nature
    (ingredients, science, facts), Dylan's Candy Bar (nutrition, ingredients), and Gathre
    (dimensions, materials, disclaimers).
15. Cross-sells are pattern-based rather than random. Rumpl shows matching designs so
    shoppers "build a kit"; FRYE shows pieces from the same style family "so the shopper
    instinctively imagines a full outfit"; BRUNT calls its module "complete your uniform,"
    which "makes the upsell feel helpful instead of pushy."
16. A single clear promise replaces a complicated explanation. Balance of Nature: "31 fruits
    and veggies without the hassle." Pepper: "no more cup gaps." Modern Mammals: "shampoo is
    bad, we make the good stuff."
17. Where a category invites disbelief, the product page carries the education. Modern
    Mammals reframes with a historical claim then positions itself as the upgrade.

## What was shown that contradicts what was said, and a cross-source conflict

**Accordions: direct conflict with B4.** Claim 14 has three brands praised for putting
details in accordions and dropdowns. Kurt Elster in B4 lists "not hiding all the content
behind accordion menus and tabs" as a property of high-converting sites. Genuine
disagreement. My read: the two are describing different content. Kurt is arguing against
hiding the *selling* copy; Shogun is describing *reference* detail (nutrition panels,
dimensions, materials) that only some shoppers want. That reading also resolves the tension
with Peculiar People's collapsed temple facts, which are reference content by nature.
Recording it as a CONFLICT with that reasoning attached rather than pretending it away.

**Nothing here is evidence.** This is a page-builder company's marketing video. No metrics,
no tests, no before-and-after, no criticism of any brand. Every claim is an assertion that
a design decision is good, with a plausible-sounding mechanism attached. Several of the
featured brands are Shogun's own customers, though the video does not say which. Treat the
observations of what is on these pages as reliable, and every causal claim as unsupported.

**The narration is machine-generated and gets details wrong.** "Frier" for FRYE, "Adenola"
for Adanola, "Rumple" for Rumpl, "Ollipop" for OLIPOP, "Peppa" for Pepper, "Gather" for
Gathre, "organized by RO" for "organized by role." A script no human proofread. It does not
invalidate the screen content, which I read directly, but it is a reason not to lean on any
specific wording here.

**Two of the four apparel brands are shown mid-discount.** Glade's homepage is "Black Friday
is Here, up to 65% off sitewide," BRUNT's is "up to $100 off sitewide," and Gathre's is
"Black Friday stacked savings." A video whose best line is that clean visual systems beat
discounts is illustrated largely with discount heroes. Worth naming, because it shows how
default the discount hero is even among stores held up as exemplary.

## Relevance to Peculiar People

- **Claim 1 is the single most actionable finding in the whole watchlist for this store,
  and it is free.** Peculiar People sells one design across roughly 167 products generated
  by an automated pipeline. If every product image is composed identically, the catalogue
  reads as a system and therefore as a real operation. If angles, crops, garment positions
  or lighting drift between temples, the grid reads as assembled rather than made. This is
  checkable on a collection page in a single screenshot, and it is exactly the kind of thing
  a pipeline can enforce permanently once it is right.
- **Claim 2 is the argument for holding the price.** BRAND.md records that Evan is holding
  premium pricing and worries it is a barrier with no reviews. This says the lever that
  substitutes for reviews is visual coherence, not markdown. It is an assertion rather than
  evidence, but it is the right assertion for the position the brand has taken.
- Claim 8 now has three independent sources (B2, B3, C2) and clears the CONFIRMED bar.
- Claim 10 is a useful correction to the obvious response to the mockup problem. The answer
  is not glossier mockups, it is photographs that show scale and real use. That agrees with
  C1's flat-lay suggestion and with A2's "imagination gap."
- Claim 11 is a live question, not an answer. Every example organises by how people shop:
  by trade, by occasion, by activity, by audience. Peculiar People organises by building.
  The question worth asking is whether a buyer arrives wanting "a temple shirt" or wanting
  "the Logan temple," and BRAND.md is explicit that this is unknown, since nobody has ever
  bought anything. Do not resolve this from a video.
- Claim 13 maps onto something the store already has. The founder intro is emotional
  content and the temple facts are rational content. Whether they are in that order is
  checkable.
- Claim 15 is the multi-temple order idea from BRAND.md in a different vocabulary, and
  suggests the framing matters: "complete your uniform" rather than a discount.
