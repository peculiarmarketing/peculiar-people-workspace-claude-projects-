# C3. The Perfect $20,000/Month Shopify Store Design (Copy This!)

## Source
- Channel: Jack Wilson, Section Store. He sells Shopify sections and templates.
- Length 5:19, published 9 May 2026
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zyr15dQIhTE
- Watched: 00:00 to 03:05, balanced detail, 31 scene-selected frames, native captions.
- Stopped at 03:05, before the install pitch, as planned. From about 02:47 the video is a
  walkthrough of buying and installing his $12 template.

The store shown is a demo he built ("Wellness Garden"), not a trading business.

## Testable claims

The premium-look argument
1. **"Expensive-looking premium ecommerce isn't about adding more."** His stated central
   point, and the most quotable formulation of the look-established problem from the
   opposite direction to the usual advice.
2. The hero uses minimal text, one call to action, and a lot of white space, letting the
   image do the work.
3. He names the failure mode directly: "your hero section doesn't need to be designed like
   Amazon with about a billion different colours and things to click on." He shows an
   Amazon homepage on screen as the anti-example.
4. Products appear immediately after the hero, grouped into a small number of clean,
   named categories.
5. Product cards have a hover animation and a call to action through to the product page.
   (Independently matches B3's hover-state finding.)

Homepage rhythm
6. **The homepage alternates section formats rather than stacking product grids.** His list:
   products, lifestyle imagery, split layouts, campaign-style sections, user content. His
   stated reason: "it keeps the homepage feeling interesting without being chaotic."
7. The mechanism he claims: a homepage built this way "is like a brand campaign or an
   editorial website instead of a catalog, and that subconsciously builds the customer's
   trust." The alternative he names is "an endless product grid stepped on top of one
   another."

Coherence over components
8. **"A lot of Shopify stores feel a little bit stitched together because we're using a ton
   of random different apps, one for reviews, one for videos, one for products, and all
   these components from different places."** He avoids that deliberately. This is the
   sharpest statement of a mechanism nothing else on the watchlist named: the visual
   incoherence that comes from assembling a store out of third-party widgets that were each
   designed by someone else.
9. Social proof is styled to match the brand rather than dropped in as a widget. His words:
   "I don't want to ruin the homepage with a random reviews widget, or just chuck in a bunch
   of really bright off-brand videos in here." He cites Rhode and Kylie Cosmetics as premium
   brands that do this.

## Apparel-specific

None. The demo is a wellness brand. Claims 1, 2, 6, 7, 8 and 9 are brand-perception claims
that carry across categories.

## What was shown that contradicts what was said

**The whole video is an advertisement, and the headline is doing work the content cannot.**
The title says $20,000/month; the opening line says the homepage "looks like it costs about
$20,000 to build." Those are different claims and the video only supports the second, which
is itself an aesthetic judgment about a store he built himself to demonstrate a template he
sells. No store, no revenue, no data.

**"From my testing" and "they're working extremely well" are never evidenced.** He says he
has built hundreds of Shopify sites and has been testing these layouts across his brands
for months. No metric, no comparison, no named brand.

**A tension he does not acknowledge.** His argument is that stores look cheap when they are
assembled from third-party components (claim 8), and his solution is to buy a $12
third-party template and install it in two clicks. That is a coherent product, but it sits
oddly with claim 8's diagnosis and with C1 and B2's finding that unmodified theme defaults
are exactly what makes a store read as generic. A template bought by many stores becomes the
new default.

**The demo has no content.** Every element is placeholder text and stock photography. He is
demonstrating a layout, not a brand, which is fine for his purpose and means nothing here
can be checked against a real customer's experience.

## Statistics stated

None. The $20,000 is a hypothetical agency price, not a measurement.

## Relevance to Peculiar People

Weakest source on the watchlist by evidence, and it earns its place on one idea.

- **Claim 8 is the most useful thing in it.** BRAND.md records that navigation runs through
  an Easify dropdown app on every product page, that the size guide is a video, and that
  various pieces are assembled by an automated pipeline. That is precisely the "stitched
  together from components each designed by someone else" pattern. Whether the Easify
  dropdown looks like it belongs to the brand or looks like an app is a specific, visual,
  checkable question, and nothing else in the research raised it.
- **Claims 1, 2 and 7 support the positioning BRAND.md already chose.** Premium, reverent,
  understated is a restraint strategy, and this is the one source arguing restraint reads
  as expensive. It pairs with B4's warning that restraint executed badly reads as
  unreadable. Together they bound the problem: strip the page down, but do not make the
  type small and grey while doing it.
- **Claim 6 is a genuine open question for this store and should not be treated as settled.**
  Peculiar People has roughly 167 near-identical products differing only by temple. An
  endless product grid is the natural output of that catalogue, and it is the exact pattern
  he says reads as a catalogue rather than a brand. But the alternative he prescribes needs
  lifestyle imagery and campaign photography that does not exist and is blocked on the
  garment decision. Worth flagging as a real constraint rather than a fixable finding.
- Claim 9 is not yet actionable. There is no social proof to style.
