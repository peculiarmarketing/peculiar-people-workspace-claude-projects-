# B3. How Fashion Ecom Brands Can Increase their Website Conversions (& AOV)

## Source
- Channel: Ross Davies, of Strafe Creative
- Length 19:06, published 4 Jun 2024
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YSxasDHumY
- Watched: whole video, balanced detail, 45 scene-selected frames, native captions.

A walkthrough of Fashion Nova on mobile first, then desktop. He states up front that he is
not their agency and is auditing from the outside. He skips the category page deliberately
and says so.

## Testable claims

Above the fold on the product page
1. Everything needed to buy is visible without scrolling: imagery, price, reviews, colour
   selector, size selector, size guide, add to bag. He enumerates that list explicitly.
2. A sale price shows the discount and the original, with colour contrast on the
   discount marker.
3. A breadcrumb sits at the top of the product page.

Fit and sizing, the strongest part of this source
4. **The page states the model's height and the size they are wearing.** His example: "this
   model is 5'5", she's wearing this size."
5. **Where the garment fits unusually, the page says so through the model.** His example:
   "this model here is a size 10, but actually they're wearing a size 12 because it's very
   tight fitting." He says this both raises conversion and lowers returns.
6. Reviews carry a fit dimension, not just a star rating. Fashion Nova shows an overall fit
   scale, which he calls "absolutely genius."
7. **Each individual review states the size that reviewer bought and the size they normally
   wear.** His example: "size purchased is a small, and normally a small to medium."

Objection handling
8. The page answers the practical garment questions: fabric, how it fastens, how it is
   washed, whether it needs ironing. He marks Fashion Nova down for not doing this: "it
   doesn't necessarily give me that information and I do think that's probably an error."

Sticky add to cart
9. There is a sticky add-to-cart that appears once the user scrolls past the main one,
   carrying a thumbnail, the title, the price and the button.
10. **On mobile the sticky bar belongs at the bottom of the screen, because that is where
    the thumb is,** and because the size selector will open from the bottom anyway. He
    notes Fashion Nova puts theirs at the top, says top draws more attention, and frames it
    honestly as a thing to test rather than a settled rule.
11. His reasoning for having one at all: "you only have that split second when someone might
    buy it, and if they have to scroll all the way back up to the top, you might lose them."

Navigation and search
12. He names three ways people navigate: the menu, the search, or scrolling from wherever
    they landed. All three need to work.
13. Search results show the price. He criticises Fashion Nova for search results with no
    price, forcing a click to find out.
14. Search results prioritise products over categories and pages.
15. On desktop, product cards have a hover state, such as a quick-add button appearing.
    His reasoning: hover tells the user what is interactive. A design built mobile-first and
    ported to desktop loses this, and he says Fashion Nova has exactly that problem.

Cart
16. The cart shows a free shipping threshold with a visual fill indicator.
17. The cart offers an "add items" route back to shopping.
18. The proceed-to-checkout button is sticky at the bottom.
19. Express payment options are above the fold in the cart. He flags that on Fashion Nova
    they fall just below it on smaller phones.
20. The discount code field should not be the most visually prominent element in the cart.
    He questions its green styling: "does that need to be the main thing on this page?"

Visual hierarchy
21. He describes an escalation trap: once one element is made loud to stand out, the next
    must be louder still, and the page ends up shouting. He connects Fashion Nova's very
    bright red to this, and says it "does probably make everything feel a little bit cheap,"
    then immediately concedes "but also if you look at the prices, they are cheap, so maybe
    that's fine." A rare moment of a reviewer checking his aesthetic judgment against the
    brand's actual positioning.

## Apparel-specific, called out by name

This is the most apparel-specific source in the whole watchlist. Claims 4 to 8 are all about
the one question apparel has to answer and other categories do not: will this fit me. Three
distinct mechanisms, all independent of each other:
- the model's own measurements and worn size,
- a fit scale aggregated across reviews,
- the size bought and normally worn, on each individual review.

Claim 5 is the most useful and the least commonly implemented: use the model to communicate
that a garment runs small or large, rather than relying on the buyer to interpret a table.

## What was shown that contradicts what was said

**The title promises 10x and the video never supports it.** He opens saying the nine
insights "could potentially 10x the conversion rates on your website." Nothing in nineteen
minutes is a measured result. To his credit the framing throughout is hedged with "I would
test," "I do wonder if," and "they've probably done some testing on that," which is the
opposite of A2 and A5's confidence. The headline number is marketing; the body is careful.

**He repeatedly credits Fashion Nova with testing he has not seen.** On the breadcrumb, on
the top-positioned sticky bar, and on the search hierarchy he assumes they must have data.
That is a reasonable inference for a large retailer and he flags it as an assumption each
time, but it means several of his positive calls are deference rather than evidence.

**He never reaches an actual checkout form.** He clicks proceed to checkout, observes it is
"a very standard Shopify section," notes "there's not a whole lot of editing that you can
do," and stops. Third source in a row to stop at the cart.

**Fashion Nova is a poor model for a premium brand.** The homepage he analyses is built on
"40-90% OFF", "$5 $7 $9 $12 STEALS" and "90% off" tiles. He acknowledges the cheapness
directly in claim 21. The structural findings transfer; the visual language is the opposite
of what BRAND.md wants.

## Statistics stated

None beyond the unsupported 10x in the opening. Nothing to carry or discard.

## Relevance to Peculiar People

- Claims 4, 5, 6 and 7 are the answer to a problem BRAND.md already has on the table.
  Evan's own read on the current blanks is that Comfort Colors 1566 and 1567 "feel a bit
  light and stiff," and the whole Tapstitch sampling exists because fit and feel matter.
  None of that reaches the buyer today. The measurements table is trimmed at assembly and
  the size guide is a video.
- Claim 4 is achievable now with no models and no customers. A mockup can still be captioned
  with the garment size shown. This is the second source to say it, after B2. Two
  independent sources, so it clears the CONFIRMED bar.
- Claims 6 and 7 are blocked on having reviews at all.
- Claim 8 is partly solved already and partly not. The stored product intros carry garment
  information, but BRAND.md's rule that stored assets are copied byte-identical means the
  question is whether fabric, wash and care actually appear, not whether they could.
- Claim 9 and 10 are directly checkable and cost nothing.
- Claim 13 is worth checking. Does the store's search return anything useful at all? With
  167 products named "Essential Temple Tee (Logan)" and navigation running through an
  Easify dropdown rather than collections, search behaviour is an open question.
- Claim 15 matters more here than for most stores. If the theme is mobile-first with no
  desktop hover states, the desktop experience will feel inert.
- Claims 16 to 20 are cart mechanics tied to free shipping thresholds and discount codes.
  Both collide with print-on-demand margin and with the no-discounting position.
