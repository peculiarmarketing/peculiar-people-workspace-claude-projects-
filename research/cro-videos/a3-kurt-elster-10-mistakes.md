## Testable claims

Checkout
1. The checkout carries the brand's logo. Kurt calls an unstyled checkout the single most
   common mistake, and describes stores that cost $100,000 to build dropping to a plain
   text logo at checkout. He puts the fix at 60 seconds in checkout settings.

Navigation
2. The main menu contains only products, collections and landing pages.
3. The test, which Jay attributes to Kurt: "everything in your main menu should lead to
   money." An About page does not, so it belongs in the footer.
4. Two named failure shapes: a menu of about three items that starts with Home
   (Home, Shop, Blog, Contact, About), or a menu of about 20 items. In both, the actual
   shopping is buried under a nested dropdown.
5. The menu includes a route to what is popular and a route to what is on sale.

Product page
6. Product photography includes action shots, meaning the product in use, not only
   on-white studio shots. Kurt: "I want action shots so badly on every Shopify store."
7. The gallery includes video.
8. Product photos are annotated, with callouts pointing at specific features.
9. The description is layered rather than a single short block: a subheading, a pull quote
   from a customer review, a narrative description, bullet points, then a longer
   description, then technical detail.
10. Dimensions are given, and the page shows what those dimensions mean in practice. His
    worked example is a handbag: inner and outer dimensions, what fits inside, and a
    photograph of a person carrying it so the viewer can judge scale and styling.
11. The test for the whole page: "if your product page was a landing page, would it work?"
    If you would not run paid traffic to it as-is, it is not finished.
12. Customer social proof is placed inside the image gallery, not only at the bottom of the
    page. Jay's specific mechanic: five product photos, then a screenshot of an Instagram
    post and its comments, sitting in the gallery carousel. His reasoning is that a
    measurable share of visitors never scroll past the fold, so proof placed at the bottom
    is never seen by them.

Brand credibility (the section most relevant to Evan's problem)
13. The About page shows the founder as a person. Kurt: "I want the person not the brand."
    Video if possible.
14. There is a physical address in the footer.
15. There is a phone number that can be called.
16. The About page is not copy-pasted from ChatGPT. Kurt's reasoning is that an about page
    is no longer table stakes now that anyone can generate one, so the bar has moved to
    evidence of a real person.
17. Named dropshipper tells, to be absent: countdown timers, lightning bolt emojis,
    "sale ends in 10 minutes," "only one left in stock." Kurt calls fake urgency and fake
    scarcity flatly illegal.
18. The credibility signals are cumulative. Kurt: "at some point it trips a trigger in our
    brain and go, this is a real business." He is explicit that he does not know where the
    line sits and that it differs per person.
19. The judgment happens in about two seconds of landing. The questions Kurt names the
    brain asking: is this legit, are they real, is there any concern here, are they
    authentic.

## Apparel-specific

Nothing apparel by name. The closest is the handbag example at claim 10, which is the
size-and-scale problem in a different category: give the measurements, then show a human
holding the object so the number means something. That transfers directly to garment fit.

co-founded Bold Commerce, which sells subscription software. The subscription segment runs
about seven minutes and is the least trustworthy stretch of the episode. It is also
irrelevant here, since a temple tee is not a consumable.

## Statistics stated (single source)

- About half of visitors landing on a product page start by looking through the photos.
- Subscription programmes offering only a discount see 3 to 5 month average life and
  8 to 10% churn.
- 92% of subscribers want more one-time add-on offers.

None carried forward without a second source.
