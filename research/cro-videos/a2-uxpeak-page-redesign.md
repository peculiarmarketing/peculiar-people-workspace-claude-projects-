# A2. Ecommerce Page Redesign: UX/UI Tips to 3.5x Conversion

## Source
- Channel: uxpeak
- Length 8:22, published 25 Apr 2026, ~60k views
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oYskl2ZBoBc
- Watched: whole video, balanced detail, 46 scene-selected frames, native captions.

One supplement product page rebuilt element by element, before and after. The densest
single source on the list for above-the-fold structure.

## Testable claims

1. A status badge sits directly above the product title (best seller, new arrival, or
   top rated). His reasoning is the halo effect: it frames perception before any detail
   is read.
2. The hero image shows the product in use, not the product alone on a plain background.
   He names the problem the "imagination gap": the shopper cannot pick the item up, so
   the image has to close the distance between the object and the experience of it. His
   fix was to put the tub next to a poured glass and cut fruit.
3. Review counts are specific, not rounded. "4.9 stars, 221 reviews" instead of
   "200+ Reviews", because round numbers read as estimates or as fake.
4. Variant options are laid out in the open as visual swatches, never hidden inside a
   dropdown. He calls dropdowns "lazy design" because they make the user click, scroll and
   read just to learn what the basic choices are.
5. Each variant swatch carries a small image, not only a word.
6. Hovering a variant reveals a one-line description answering the fear attached to that
   choice. His example on a flavour: "Light, tart, and not overly sweet."
7. Purchase options are side-by-side cards, not stacked radio buttons. His reasoning:
   radio buttons present two visually equal choices, so users default to the lowest
   immediate risk.
8. The preferred option is preselected, tinted, and tagged (his tag: "Most popular").
9. The reassurance that defuses the objection sits inside the option box, not elsewhere on
   the page. His example, inside the subscribe card: save 15%, cancel anytime,
   priority dispatch.
10. Extra choices appear only after the user clicks, rather than cluttering the first view.
    He calls this progressive disclosure. His example: clicking one-time purchase expands
    to reveal 1, 2 and 3 month bundles at 0%, 10% and 20% off.
11. Button copy is not purely transactional. He changed "Add to cart" to
    "Add to cart, start my journey."
12. The reassurance icons under the button are specific to what this buyer actually fears,
    not generic. He replaced free shipping / money back / made in USA with 100% vegan,
    60-day guarantee, and third-party tested for heavy metals.
13. Element order changes results even when every element is the same size and colour.
    (Stated on a slide titled "Getting the order right.")

## What was shown that contradicts what was said

**The headline result is not a result.** The title promises 3.5x conversion and the video
opens with "one of these pages converts at 2%, the other converts at 7%." Nothing on screen
supports that. There is no test, no traffic split, no analytics view, no client named. The
"before" page is a mockup the channel appears to have built in order to improve it, and the
brand "Nourish Greens" does not appear to be a trading store. This is a design exercise
presented in the grammar of a case study. Every claim above should be read as a designer's
argument, not as measured evidence. This single fact is the most important thing about
this source.

**Trust badges, direct contradiction with A1.** He says at 7:21 that "generic badges like
free shipping don't build deep trust anymore because shoppers expect them," and removes
free shipping / money back / made in USA. A1, Shopify's own channel, recommends adding
exactly those. Straight conflict between two sources. Log it.

**"500+ sold this week."** He adds a fire icon reading "500+ sold this week" and calls it a
trust anchor. Two problems. First, he had just finished arguing that round numbers such as
500 read as fake, then used 500 as his own example. He contradicts his own rule inside 30
seconds. Second, this is a fabricated scarcity signal unless the number is real.

**Sponsor.** Mobbin sponsors the video and the mid-roll runs about 60 seconds. The design
argument itself does not depend on the sponsor, but the "study what already converts"
framing is the sponsor's pitch.

## Statistics stated (single source, do not carry forward without a second source)

- 2% versus 7% conversion, "3.5 times more revenue." Unsupported, see above.

## Relevance to Peculiar People

- Directly applicable and free: hero image showing the garment worn rather than floating,
  variant swatches instead of a dropdown, colour swatches with images, purchase options as
  cards, reassurance placed inside the box where the doubt occurs, progressive disclosure,
  specific reassurance icons rather than generic ones, element order.
- The variant-swatch rule matters a lot here. A store selling one design in 52 colourways
  lives or dies on how the colour picker is presented.
- The "imagination gap" argument is the sharpest framing found so far for the mockup
  problem in BRAND.md. Every product image is a mockup of a garment nobody is wearing,
  which is precisely the isolated-product-on-blank-background pattern he identifies as the
  thing to fix.
- Blocked: specific review counts, "sold this week" counters, best-seller badges. All
  require sales that have not happened. A best-seller badge on a store with zero orders is
  a false statement, not a design decision.
- Conflicts with brand: "500+ sold this week" style urgency, and the fabricated status
  badge. Both are ruled out by BRAND.md's guardrails and by simple honesty.
- Bundle tiers at 10% and 20% off collide with print-on-demand economics. There is no
  volume cost break, so a multi-buy discount comes straight out of margin. The
  multi-temple order idea in BRAND.md is the same shape, so the mechanic is worth keeping
  and the discount depth is the open question.
