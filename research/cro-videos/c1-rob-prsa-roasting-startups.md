# C1. ROASTING Clothing Brand Startups, Brutal Feedback

## Source
- Channel: Rob Prsa (Apparel Success). Founder of the Canadian apparel brand Kay Bahd.
- Length 22:21, published 1 Apr 2025, ~12k views
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZqhStcjZqug
- Watched: whole video, balanced detail, 48 scene-selected frames, native captions.

Three real, small, trading clothing brands reviewed with their permission, with revenue
disclosed. This is the only source in the entire watchlist that looks at brands at
Peculiar People's actual scale rather than at Bonobos, Fashion Nova or Allbirds.

- **Stvrt**, mental health and self-love apparel, one year old, about $400/month.
- **Broken Day Clothing Co**, UK metal-scene apparel, about eight months old, about
  $650/month.
- Kay Bahd, his own brand, referenced as the comparison.

He rates each brand on four categories: brand concept, website quality, social media,
products.

## Testable claims

Credibility and the "not legit" judgment
1. **Generic mockups destroy credibility.** His central claim, stated directly: "when you
   come across a brand where it just looks really generic, it hurts your credibility, it
   just feels like it's not legit, like the product quality is not going to be that good
   when they receive it in the mail." The inference runs from image quality to expected
   product quality, and the visitor makes it before buying anything.
2. Two named fixes for that, in his order of preference: buy higher quality mockup files,
   or "just lay your products down on a table, get a nice white light, and take the
   pictures yourselves." He says self-shot flat lays "will look way better on your site
   than your current mockups."
3. **Product photography sits on a plain, clean background so the edges of the garment are
   visible.** He tells Broken Day their busy decorative backgrounds mean "I have a hard time
   seeing the edges of the products and really getting a feel for the quality of the
   product." He cites Indestructible MFG as the counter-example, always on a simple white
   background, "because you can see the edges of the product."
4. He catches a specific and easily-made error at Broken Day: the *hover* image is the clean
   product shot, and the default image is the decorative one. "That's the photo that I'd use
   to start on all of your pictures... it does a way better job communicating the quality."
   The good photograph existed and was hidden behind an interaction.

Homepage copy
5. **Hero copy is not a description of the catalogue.** He rejects "browse our selection of
   mental health and self-love apparel" as "way too generic sounding" and "playing it safe."
   His suggested shapes: start with "Inspired by..." or "Representing..." rather than
   "Browse our selection of..."
6. **Section headings are not left as theme defaults.** He flags "Current Collection" and
   says it "sounds like it was just the stock text that was there inside of your theme that
   you just went with." Independent of B2, who identified an unmodified default font as the
   same class of tell.
7. The hero image is specific to the brand, not a stock landscape. He calls a generic
   mountain photograph "a perfect example of playing it safe."

Layout
8. Page sections follow a logical order. He criticises Broken Day's homepage as "too chaotic
   and sporadic," describing a hoodie section, then a random photo, then two products, then
   a newsletter block, and says fixing the order would "make it just feel a little bit more
   legit."
9. A newsletter block dropped at the bottom of the page reads as generic.

Brand concept
10. A brand in a crowded category needs a specific angle, not a category. His economic
    framing: an abundance of near-identical brands "dilutes the value of all of these
    brands." His own worked example is narrowing from "Canadian lifestyle brand" to the
    slang and humour of rural Canada.
11. He praises Broken Day precisely for the opposite of Stvrt's problem: "I love the
    specificity of your brand concept, the fact that you're diving so hard into a particular
    niche."
12. Designs that carry a saying connect better than designs that only carry the brand's
    logo. He references the And1 strategy against a plain Nike swoosh.

Content
13. Heavy video effects read as gimmicky and reduce perceived realness. He tells Broken Day
    that plain footage of a model wearing the shirt with a close-up on the print "might do
    even better than all these really cool visual effects."
14. Most content should be top of funnel, about the subject the audience cares about, with
    the product merely present, rather than promotional.

## What was shown that contradicts what was said

**He recommends a mockup vendor while arguing mockups are the problem.** Claim 1 says
generic mockups make a brand read as illegitimate. His first fix is a different mockup
site (yellowimages.com), and his second, offered almost as an afterthought, is to
photograph the real garments. His own diagnosis argues the second fix is the real one:
better mockups are still mockups. He half-recognises this by suggesting the flat lay, then
moves on.

**He is honest about the limits of his taste.** On Broken Day's backgrounds: "I'm aware that
this might just be me and maybe your audience is resonating with it and you don't want to
change it, that's completely cool." He separates his preference from a claim about
performance, which most of this genre does not do.

**He rates a site 7/10 on "website quality" while never opening the cart or checkout.**
The Stvrt product page is on screen at 8:15 showing the exact Shopify default that B2
identified as a problem, an outlined "Add to cart" sitting under a solid purple "Buy with
Shop Pay". He does not mention it. His review is a brand review that includes a website,
not a conversion audit.

**Sponsored by an AI logo generator.** The mid-roll is for design.com, an AI logo tool, and
the chapter list includes "Ai Logo Designs." A video partly about brands looking generic is
sponsored by a tool that generates logos from templates. Worth naming.

**The revenue numbers are self-reported by the brands** and unverified.

## Statistics stated

- Stvrt: one year in business, roughly $400/month.
- Broken Day: since August 2024, roughly $650/month.
- Kay Bahd: struggled for six months, reached about $70,000/month at four to five years.
Self-reported, single source, not carried forward. The useful part is not the numbers but
the shape: two real brands with real products and real content were making a few hundred
dollars a month after a year.

## Relevance to Peculiar People

This is the source that speaks most directly to the brief, and it delivers one hard finding
and one uncomfortable piece of context.

- **Claim 1 is the answer to Evan's stated problem, and it lands on the mockups.** Every
  product image on peculiarpeopleco.com is a mockup, which BRAND.md states plainly. This is
  now the fifth independent source to arrive at product imagery, and the only one to name
  the exact mechanism Evan described: generic imagery makes a visitor infer the product will
  be poor quality when it arrives.
- **Claim 2 is achievable now and does not wait on the Tapstitch decision.** Evan already
  receives sample garments; BRAND.md describes "The Inspection" as a content show built on
  photographing delivered samples. A white background and a flat lay of a garment he already
  owns is not blocked by anything. The one caution is BRAND.md's rule not to build content
  showing products that may be discontinued, which applies to elaborate lifestyle shoots
  more than to a plain flat lay.
- **Claim 3 conflicts with nothing and confirms something.** Plain background, garment edges
  visible. Peculiar People's constraint is the reverse of Broken Day's: the art is a back
  print, so the garment must be shown from behind, and the print must be legible against the
  colourway.
- Claims 5, 6 and 7 are directly checkable copy and imagery decisions costing nothing.
  Claim 6 pairs with B2's default-font finding as the same tell: unedited theme defaults.
- Claim 10 has already been decided in Peculiar People's favour. The brand is not "faith
  apparel"; it is line drawings of specific temples with verified histories. BRAND.md calls
  the research library an underused asset that nobody else in the category has. By Rob's
  own test the brand concept is closer to Broken Day than to Stvrt, and the differentiation
  problem he spends most of the video on is not this store's problem.
- Claim 12 touches an open decision. BRAND.md lists a "Be Peculiar" wordmark line and
  records that 1 Peter 2:9 in product copy is TBD. Rob's argument is that a saying
  outperforms a bare logo. Relevant input, not a decision.
- **The uncomfortable context.** Two brands with finished products, active social accounts
  and functioning websites were making $400 and $650 a month after roughly a year. BRAND.md
  already says distribution is the only constraint and that the product side is done. This
  video is evidence for that position, not against it. A CRO audit can remove friction and
  raise credibility, and it cannot manufacture visitors.
