# B2. You Won't Believe What I Found Reviewing Your Clothing Brand

## Source
- Channel: BitBranding. Presenter is Christian Pon, who describes himself as a Shopify expert.
- Length 15:50, published 22 Aug 2024
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oWi7ZtMAA4E
- Watched: whole video, balanced detail, 48 frames, native captions.

Three subscriber-submitted clothing stores reviewed live on screen:
- **Silva Espadas**, graphic tees. The closest analogue to Peculiar People found anywhere in
  this research: a small store selling printed tees on a stock Shopify theme.
- **Divalo**, luxury womenswear. Used as the positive example.
- **Ell Soll Fashion**, fast fashion.

## Testable claims

Theme and brand surface
1. The theme's default font is changed. He identifies the first store as unmodified Dawn on
   sight: "you're using Dawn, but then the font is still the default font for Dawn."
2. There is a logo, not just the shop name set in text.
3. Colours are consistent. He criticises a blue announcement bar whose colour appears
   nowhere else on the page, next to a red element and a third blue in the cart icon.
4. The homepage does more than list products. His framing: the homepage is a business card,
   and "from a business card you should be able to tell who you are, what you offer, what
   you do." He explicitly rejects the excuse that a small catalogue justifies a bare
   homepage.

Floating widgets and popups
5. Floating corner widgets do not overlap each other. On the first store a "get 10% off"
   tab sits on top of the cart bubble. He notes desktop tolerates one or two, mobile does not.
6. A cookie banner and an email popup do not fire at the same time. He demonstrates the
   failure live: accepting the cookie banner also dismissed the email popup, so the popup
   never got its chance.
7. Nothing above the fold moves or auto-scrolls. "You don't want to distract your customer
   with all these different things, all these moving parts."

Product page
8. **The size selector is buttons, not a dropdown.** His reasoning is click count: a
   dropdown means click, choose, then add to cart, where buttons mean click then add.
9. **Add to cart is the most prominent button on the page.** He tells the first store to
   remove or demote the "Buy with Shop Pay" button, which the Shopify default renders as a
   solid purple block above a plain outlined Add to Cart, so the theme's own default makes
   the wrong button dominant.
10. Payment method icons go below the buttons, not as a competing button.
11. The product page states what size the model is wearing.
12. The product page states where the item is made.
13. The product page carries shipping and returns information.
14. There is a size guide.
15. Trust badges appear on every page, not only the homepage. He flags Divalo for having
    them in the footer but absent from the product page.

Footer and credibility
16. The footer carries both legal pages and company pages: our story, sustainability, terms,
    privacy, shipping and returns. He is explicit that the presence of these pages is itself
    a trust signal, independent of anyone reading them.
17. "Secure payments" is a weak badge because every store has it. He recommends replacing it
    with something that actually differentiates.
18. **Remove the country selector if you do not ship to those countries.** He is blunt about
    why: a new store listing Belgium, Bolivia, Brazil and Chad is pretending to be a
    worldwide company. "What worldwide company? It's like, not really." He also notes it
    slows the site.
19. The email signup is an inline field, not a button that opens another popup.

Cart drawer
20. The cart confirms free shipping has been earned and states the returns window.
21. The cart shows recommended products.

## Apparel-specific, called out by name

Claims 8, 11, 12 and 14 are apparel reasoning. Claim 11 in particular is a finding that has
not appeared anywhere else in the research: **state the model's size**, so a viewer can
calibrate the fit they are looking at against their own body. That is the missing half of a
size guide. A measurements table tells you the garment's dimensions; the model's size tells
you what those dimensions look like on a person.

## What was shown that contradicts what was said

**He praises badges that A2 says are worthless.** He calls Divalo's free express shipping
and free returns badges "great" and singles out only "secure payments" as weak. A2 removed
free shipping, money back and made in USA as generic badges shoppers now discount. Partial
conflict. The reconciliation that fits both: the objection is to badges that state the
universal minimum, not to badges that state a real policy. Free shipping over $150 is a
policy; "secure payments" is a claim every store can make. That reading is consistent with
A1's evidence too, where the established apparel stores showed policy text rather than
badge clusters.

**He does not verify a broken page.** Divalo's cart drawer fails to load its recommended
products on camera and he says "maybe it's my internet, I don't know," then recommends the
feature anyway. He never reloads to check. A real audit would have.

**He never reaches a checkout.** Three stores reviewed, no checkout opened on any of them.
The genre consistently stops at the cart, which is a gap this research cannot close from
video alone.

**Unverified credentials claim.** He opens by saying BitBranding helped "over 250 clothing
brands" add "more than 10 million in extra revenue" in the past year. No evidence offered.
This is a lead-generation video: the call to action is to comment to get your site audited.

## Statistics stated

Only the 250 brands / $10 million agency claim above. Nothing measurable about page design.

## Relevance to Peculiar People

This is the most transferable source in the research, because Silva Espadas is almost
exactly Peculiar People's situation: printed graphic tees, small catalogue, stock theme,
one design per product.

- Claim 1 is a direct check. If Peculiar People runs a stock theme with untouched default
  type, a Shopify expert identified that on sight in about three seconds. That is precisely
  the "does not look established" problem stated as something checkable.
- Claim 9 matters and is easy to miss. The Shopify default puts a solid Shop Pay button
  above a plain outlined Add to Cart. Two things follow: the visual hierarchy is wrong, and
  the more prominent button is branded Shop Pay rather than the store. Worth auditing.
- Claim 8 corroborates A2's anti-dropdown rule from an apparel direction, and independently
  matches B1's push to get size controls into the open above the fold. Three sources now.
- Claim 11 is free, achievable today, and nothing blocks it. Peculiar People has no models,
  but the same principle applies to a mockup: state what size garment is shown.
- Claim 18 is worth checking and costs nothing to fix.
- Claim 16 connects to A3's founder-story finding. The pages themselves are the signal.
- Claims 20 and 21 need free shipping economics and cross-sell inventory logic that
  print-on-demand complicates.
- Claim 4 is the strategic one. BRAND.md notes navigation runs through an Easify dropdown
  rather than collections, and that the store is unannounced. A homepage that only lists
  products, on a brand whose whole proposition is a story about temples and memory, is
  leaving the actual differentiator off the page.
