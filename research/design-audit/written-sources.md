# design-audit: written sources

Cross-check for the design-audit rule library, from books, foundry and studio
writing, and print vendor technical guides. Researched 7 October 2026 with
WebSearch and WebFetch from a claude.ai cloud session.

Subject being audited: a one-colour circular seal printed white on navy (#001A58)
fabric, jacket back, 10 or 12 inches wide. Thick outer ring, a band of small serif
capitals with small solid diamond dividers, a thin ring, larger bold serif caps
arched across the top and a small "EST. 2023" arched along the bottom, a heavier
inner ring, and a centre architectural line drawing. Tapstitch prints DTG on tees
and DTF on fleece; the jacket method is not yet known.

## How to read this

- **ESTABLISHED**: at least two independent sources agree (same number or
  overlapping ranges).
- **SINGLE SOURCE**: one source only. Usable as a default, not as a hard fail.
- **FOLKLORE**: widely repeated, but the sources that check it find no support.
- **CONFLICT**: sources disagree; the conflict is stated.
- "Quoted via X" means the original was not fetched; the words come from X
  (a search engine summary, a book summary, a mirror). Treat those as one step
  weaker than a direct fetch.
- Unit conversions are mine: 1 pt = 1/72 in = 0.3528 mm; 0.001 in = 0.0254 mm.

## Topic 1. Logo and emblem: simplicity, scalability, small size, one colour, distinctiveness, badge construction

**1.1 Simplicity is the means of identification.**
- W18 Rand: "The role of the logo is to point, to designate, in as simple a manner as possible." "A design that is complex, like a fussy illustration or an arcane abstraction, harbors a self-destruct mechanism."
- W20 Airey (quoted via summary): "Keep it simple. The simplest solution is often the most effective."
- W22 Frank (Viget): smoothing detailed shapes, thickening thin strokes and filling outlined elements "enhance legibility at condensed sizes".
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** (qualitative; no number).

**1.2 Must reproduce in one colour.**
- W18 Rand: "the only thing mandatory, it seems, is that a logo be attractive, reproducible in one color and in exceedingly small sizes."
- W20 Airey (quoted via summary): "you must also supply a version that uses just one [colour]"; "By refraining from using color until the end of the process..."
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED**. (Our seal is already one colour, so this rule passes by construction; the audit should instead check that nothing depends on a tint or a gradient.)

**1.3 Minimum size the mark should survive.**
- W20 Airey (quoted via book summary, not the book): "Your design should ideally work at a minimum of around one inch in size without loss of detail."
- W21 Leonardis (piccolino test): minimum viable about 1 inch wide; smallest common application 3/4 inch; "If you cannot reduce your logo smaller than two inches and still have everything remain clear, it's likely your logo is overly complex." Bonus test: 16 px favicon.
- W19 Airey (Peachpit excerpt, fetched): "If a symbol loses detail at small sizes, you can always create different versions for different measurements."
- Agreement: about 1 inch, two sources (W20, W21). Note W21 is a studio blog and may itself draw on Airey, so independence is moderate.
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** for "test at about 1 inch". For a dense seal, W19 and W22 both endorse a separate simplified small version rather than forcing the full seal to work at 1 inch: **ESTABLISHED** (W19, W22).

**1.4 Emblems with encircling text fail at small sizes; drop outer text for small versions.**
- W22 Frank: "There's A LOT of text encircling the main graphical emblem, and it would be illegible at small sizes" (NOAA example); outer text "can be progressively removed for smaller formats".
- W19 Airey: different versions for different sizes (general).
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** (W19 general, W22 specific). Relevant only if the seal is reused small (neck label, hat); at 10 to 12 inches it is not a constraint.

**1.5 Distinctiveness: one recognisable feature / silhouette.**
- W19 Airey (fetched): "All strong logos have one single feature that helps them stand out. Apple has the bite. Mercedes has the three-pointed star."
- W20 Airey (summary): "So recognizable, in fact, that just its shape or outline gives it away."
- W18 Rand: "It is easier to remember a well designed image than one that is muddled."
- Note: W19 and W20 are the same author. Rand supports memorability, not the "one feature" claim specifically.
- Verdict: **SINGLE SOURCE** (Airey) for "one single feature"; **ESTABLISHED** for "muddled is less memorable" (Rand, Airey).

**1.6 Badge construction (rings concentric, centred on a grid).**
- W16 Buculei: rings are circles "align[ed] ... to the center" of the badge; example ring stroke 3 px.
- W17 Spooner: elements aligned "centrally with the existing elements"; stroke weights used 2 pt and 5 pt.
- Verdict: concentric centring **ESTABLISHED** (trivially). Neither gives proportional rules for ring widths (see topic 6).

## Topic 2. Composition and spacing

**2.1 Optical centre sits above the mathematical centre.**
- W24 PrintWiki: "a point located slightly above the geometric center of a page."
- W25 Duke: "The optical center of a rectangular region ... is slightly above the geometric center." Also notes mat boards are wider at the bottom for this reason.
- W26 Paper Mill Store: "just slightly above the exact geometric center."
- W23 Pangram Pangram: "While software can place objects with mathematical precision, our eyes don't always agree with the result."
- **Amount:** no fetched source gives a number. W27 (forum, quoted via search summary) gives a framer's rule of thumb: nudge the centre up "1/10th of half the height", which is 5 percent of the full height. The often repeated "3 to 5 percent" or "10 percent" figures did not appear in any source I could fetch.
- Verdict: "above centre" **ESTABLISHED**; any specific percentage **SINGLE SOURCE** (W27, forum) and should not be a hard rule. For a circular seal, apply it to the centre drawing inside the inner ring: a drawing with a heavy base will look low if mathematically centred.

**2.2 Round shapes must overshoot to look the same size as flat shapes.**
- W23 Pangram Pangram: "circles in logos are often drawn slightly larger than squares they're paired with"; overshoots stop "O, C and S" looking "too small next to H, E or X".
- Monotype foundry support has a "Lack of overshoots" article (seen in results, not fetched).
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** in type design; no number given (type designers commonly use about 1 to 3 percent of cap height, but I found no fetched source for a figure, so treat any number as **FOLKLORE** here).

**2.3 Visual weight factors.**
- W28 Bradley (Smashing, via search summary): size, colour/value, density, isolation ("An element surrounded by negative space will have significantly more visual weight"), position (higher reads heavier).
- Verdict: **SINGLE SOURCE** in this check (one fetched-via-summary article). Directionally consistent with 2.1.

**2.4 Hierarchy should be visible when blurred.** See topic 8 (squint test). **ESTABLISHED.**

**2.5 Modular / ratio spacing systems.** See topic 3. Tools, not laws (W7 Brown: "modular scales are a tool, they're not magic").

## Topic 3. Ratios

**3.1 Golden ratio as a source of beauty.**
- W29/W30 Devlin (Stanford, quoted via Hyperallergic and ScienceAlert): students' preferred rectangles "are always random and frequently change"; "The golden ratio stuff is in the realm of religious belief ... it's just not fact"; people favour ratios they are used to, such as A4 or screens.
- W33 Markowsky (1992, quoted via search summary): the mathematical properties are right, but much said about the ratio in art, architecture and aesthetics "is false or seriously misleading".
- W31 Brownlee (FastCo Design, quoted via Gizmodo): the aesthetic claim "comes primarily from two people, one of whom was misquoted, and the other of whom was just making shit up" (Pacioli misattribution; Zeising's 19th-century claims, per W29).
- W30 Torrence (Randolph-Macon): "hocus-pocus".
- Verdict: **FOLKLORE**. An audit must never fail a design for not hitting 1.618.

**3.2 Famous logos "built on" the golden ratio.**
- W32 Cole on the Apple logo: curves are not true circular arcs; a measurement ambiguity of 7.5 versus 8.5 units is "the difference between having a golden ratio value of 1.53 and 1.73". Core77 summary of Cole: "real visual rhythm is hurt by precision ... if it looks right, it is right." Search summary also attributes to the designer Rob Janoff: "When I designed it I pretty much did it freehand."
- W34 Pepsi/Arnell "Breathtaking" document (2009): widely treated as absurd (golden ratio, Mona Lisa, Parthenon, magnetic fields); some think it a hoax.
- Twitter bird circles overlay: searched, no fetched debunk; it is a post-hoc overlay of the same kind.
- Verdict: **FOLKLORE** (retrofits). Treat golden-ratio overlays as decoration.

**3.3 Modular scales (what designers actually use).**
- W7 Brown: start with a size and a ratio, multiply up and divide down; golden 1:1.618 is one choice among "geometry, music, nature, and history"; "modular scales are a tool, they're not magic"; he departs from his own scale when needed. Quotes Bringhurst: "A modular scale, like a musical scale, is a prearranged set of harmonious proportions."
- W6 Mortensen: the classic printer's scale 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 18, 21, 24, 30, 36, 48, 60, 72 (as recorded by Bringhurst) fits f = f0 x r^(i/n) with r = 2 and n = 5, so each step is about 1.149 (2^(1/5)); he notes 11 pt and 30 pt do not fit and 42 pt is missing.
- Common ratios 1.25, 1.333 (4:3, perfect fourth), 1.5 (3:2, perfect fifth), 1.618: named in Brown's tool (modularscale.com) and many type-scale tools; no fetched designer source argues one is better.
- Verdict: using a consistent ratio for sizes is **ESTABLISHED** practice (W6, W7, Bringhurst via both); any specific ratio being "correct" is **FOLKLORE**.

**3.4 Rule of thirds.**
- W35 Amirshahi et al.: aesthetic ratings "correlated only weakly with subjective rule of thirds scores and not at all with calculated" values; the rule "seems to play only a minor, if any, role" in large sets of high-quality photographs and paintings.
- Verdict: **FOLKLORE** as an aesthetic law; also irrelevant to a centred circular seal.

## Topic 4. Typography

**4.1 Letterspace all caps by 5 to 12 percent of the point size.**
- W1 Butterick (verified, fetched): "you always add 5–12% extra letterspacing to text in all caps or small caps"; in CSS "0.05em to 0.12em".
- W5 Bringhurst (via webtypography.net): "The normal value for letterspacing these sequences of small or full caps is 5% to 10% of the type size."
- W12 Google Fonts Knowledge: more open tracking "usually helps with the readability of all-capital text".
- W8 Strizver (Fonts.com): "Open tracking when using a font intended for display at smaller-than-display sizes"; "Close tracking when using text designs at larger sizes."
- W2 Butterick: "Always add letterspacing to caps ... and make sure kerning is turned on."
- Agreement: 5 to 10 percent (Bringhurst) sits inside 5 to 12 percent (Butterick). Units: 5 percent of the size = 0.05 em = 50 in Illustrator/InDesign tracking units (1/1000 em).
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED**, range 5 to 10 percent safe, up to 12 percent acceptable. Apply more to the small band caps, less to the large bold top arc (W8). Straight-line figures only: circle placement changes effective spacing (4.4).

**4.2 Size steps for hierarchy.**
- W3 Butterick: "Use the smallest increment necessary to make a visible difference"; no more than three levels, two ideal; bold reads better than italic for headings.
- W6/W7: pick sizes from a consistent scale.
- No fetched source gives a minimum ratio between levels.
- Verdict: "few levels, visible but not exaggerated steps" **ESTABLISHED** (W3 plus scale sources). For the seal: three text sizes (top arc, band caps, EST. 2023) is at Butterick's maximum; a fourth would breach it. Any numeric minimum ratio is unsourced.

**4.3 Type on a circular path: baseline versus centre alignment.**
- W15 Adobe (quoted via search summary): Align to Path options are Ascender, Descender, Center ("halfway between the font's ascender and descender") and Baseline (default).
- W11 Felici: "Characters standing on the path have normal spacing where they meet the baseline, but their tops get bunched up on concave baselines and spread too far apart on convex baselines." "You'll get the most natural character spacing if you select Center from the Align menu."
- Search summary of a CreativePro TypeTalk article (403): "Setting type top-aligned with the curve badly pinches the bottoms"; "vertically centering the type on the curved path is the happy medium."
- Verdict: centre alignment gives the most even spacing on curves **ESTABLISHED** (W11, CreativePro via summary). For a seal with a band, the practical rule that follows is: top text and bottom text should be visually centred in the same band, so their cap heights sit at the same distance from the bounding rings (derived, not quoted).

**4.4 Convex (outside) text spreads, concave (inside) text crowds; compensate with tracking.**
- W9 Peters: "Type set inside a circle tends to crowd, while the letters will fan out if set outside the circle." Further from the circle is looser; nearer the centre is tighter. Example values for one face (Sabre): tracking -75 outside, +50 inside, plus kerning fixes for pairs like Th and Wo.
- W11 Felici: "tops get bunched up on concave baselines and spread too far apart on convex baselines"; in tight bends "use your tracking controls to tighten or loose the overall spacing".
- W10 Peters: font kerning tables made for straight lines can make curved text worse ("LA", "RA" gaps; "T" and "E" crash); "more than half of the letter pairs" needed manual adjustment in his example.
- Verdict: direction of the effect **ESTABLISHED** (W9, W11). Specific tracking numbers **SINGLE SOURCE** (W9, one typeface); the audit should check evenness of gaps, not a target tracking value. Manual kerning on curves **ESTABLISHED** (W10, W11).

**4.5 Bottom arc reads left to right, the right way up.**
- W16 Buculei: bottom text on a second circle, flip the circle vertically so "The text will jump automatically at the bottom", then resize and re-centre it.
- W15 Adobe / Photoshop practice (quoted via search summary): drag the text to the inside of the path so it reads right side up, then use Baseline Shift to sit the letters correctly relative to the circle.
- Kittl/Divi-type tool docs (via search summary): duplicate and use a negative radius "so it bends the other way".
- Mechanism (derived): on the top arc, letters stand on the outside of their path (convex, letters fan out). On the bottom arc, letters read upright only if their baseline is on the outer side and they hang inward, so they are concave relative to the circle centre and crowd (4.4). Hence bottom text usually needs more tracking than top text at the same size.
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** (W16, W15 via summary, tool docs). The "concave bottom text needs looser tracking" corollary is derived from W9 and W11, so it inherits their ESTABLISHED status.

**4.6 Centre each arc on the vertical axis.**
- W15 Adobe (via search summary): to centre on top, create the path type from the bottom of the circle or drag the start and end brackets "so that the center of the text rests at the top of the circle".
- CreativePro "Center text on top of a circular path" (via search summary, 403): click the insertion point at the 6 o'clock handle and set paragraph alignment to centred.
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** (two tool-procedure sources, both via summary). Audit check: the midpoint of the top arc's text sits at 12 o'clock and the bottom arc's at 6 o'clock, within a visible tolerance; optically, a word ending in a wide letter may need a nudge (derived from 2.2).

**4.7 Minimum legible type size in print and on fabric.** (Fabric numbers in 7.6.)
- W4 Butterick: body text in print 10 to 12 pt; business cards "often only 6–8 points".
- W13 Google Fonts: small optical sizes have "less stroke contrast", "more open spacing" and "taller x-heights"; so a high-contrast display serif is a poor choice for the small band caps.
- W14 Google Fonts: in print, "Dark ink tends to spread into lighter letterforms", obscuring "thin strokes in high-contrast fonts"; on screens light type looks heavier. Fix: adjust weight or grade.
- Verdict: paper text minimum around 6 pt **SINGLE SOURCE** (W4); "small caps in a seal need a low-contrast, sturdier serif" **ESTABLISHED** (W13, W14).

## Topic 5. Line work

**5.1 Keep stroke weights consistent within a set.**
- W39 Material Design (via summary): system icons use "a consistent stroke width of 2dp, including curves, angles, and both interior and exterior strokes."
- W17 Spooner and W16 Buculei both build badges from a small number of fixed stroke weights (2 pt and 5 pt; 3 px).
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** as practice (icon systems and badge tutorials), qualitative.

**5.2 Use few weights (two to four) with clear steps.**
- W36 LibreCAD (fetched): ISO pens shown as thin 0.18 mm, medium 0.35 mm, thick 0.70 mm, which is a 1:2:4 hierarchy.
- W37 FreeCAD ISO 128-20 line groups (via summary): thick = 1 x group, thin = 1/2 x group, a 2:1 ratio. ISO pen series 0.18, 0.25, 0.35, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0, 1.4, 2.0 steps by about 1.41 (square root of 2).
- W38 architecture guides (via summary): "Most drawings use three or four weights: around 0.70 mm for cut elements, 0.35 mm for seen elements, and 0.18 to 0.25 mm for hatching." Heavier for profile/cut, lighter for detail and background.
- Verdict: three weights with roughly doubling steps (1:2:4) **ESTABLISHED** in technical drawing (W36, W37, W38). Transferring it to a logo is a reasonable analogy, not a sourced logo rule. Adjacent weights closer than about 1.4x will not read as distinct (derived from the ISO step).

**5.3 Outer borders heavier than interior detail.**
- W38 (via summary): heaviest for profile/cut lines, lighter inside.
- W17 Spooner: heavier 5 pt stroke used "to anchor overlapping elements together".
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** weakly (one technical summary, one tutorial). For the seal: ring weights should rank outer thick > inner heavy > thin ring, and the centre drawing's finest line should be the thinnest thing in the mark, but still above the print minimum in topic 7.

**5.4 Convert strokes to filled shapes before print.**
- W58 Gooten (via summary): "Strokes or lines should be expanded into filled shapes to ensure line weights are preserved."
- W42 Clement: test the thinnest elements by dragging a reference circle of the minimum diameter along them (presupposes measured geometry).
- Verdict: **SINGLE SOURCE** for the explicit instruction; good practice.

## Topic 6. Circular seals and badges

**6.1 Regulated engineer seals (two concentric circles, text in the band).**

- W69 Kansas (via summary): outer 1-5/8 in, inner 1-1/16 in. Band 0.281 in = 17.3 percent; inner/outer = 0.65.

- W69 Idaho (via summary): outer 1-1/2 in, inner 15/16 in. Band 0.281 in = 18.8 percent; inner/outer = 0.63.

- Verdict: inner field about 63 to 67 percent of outer diameter, text band about 17 to 19 percent of diameter per side: **ESTABLISHED** as a convention in regulated seals (four jurisdictions, though not independent in origin). They are embossed paper seals, not apparel graphics, so use them as a reference range, not a pass/fail rule. Our seal has two text zones (outer band plus arcs inside the thin ring), so its centre field will be smaller; the audit should report the ratio rather than fail it.

**6.2 Cap height as a fraction of band width; spacing between rings; dividers.**
- No written source found. Dividers: W17 uses stars "to fill out any unused space"; a badge vendor glossary (via summary) calls dots or stars "text separators".
- Verdict: **NO SOURCE**. Any number here must come from the video SPECs or from measurement of reference seals, and should be labelled as such. A defensible derived rule: the band text should be visually centred in the band with equal clearance to the inner and outer rings (follows from 4.3).

## Topic 7. Print constraints (screen print, DTF, DTG)

Conversion table used below: 0.012 in = 0.30 mm = 0.86 pt; 0.018 in = 0.46 mm = 1.3 pt; 0.02 in = 0.51 mm = 1.44 pt; 0.04 in = 1.02 mm = 2.88 pt; 0.5 mm = 1.42 pt; 1 mm = 2.83 pt; 1 pt = 0.35 mm.

### 7.1 Screen print: minimum positive line

| Source | Minimum positive line |
|---|---|
| W40 Transfer Express (screen-printed transfers) | "recommended line thickness ... is .012″ thick for printed areas" (0.30 mm, 0.86 pt); reflective 0.25 in |
| W41 Transfer Express video | "This gets down to .012 inches or around 1 pt"; glitter ".025" or about a 1.8 pt" |
| W43 FM Expressions (spot colour) | "Line/area thickness 1 pt. or 0.01388 of an inch"; performance inks 2 pt |
| W45 Kotis Design | "1 pt positive space" |
| W48 Supacolor (transfer, art specs page) | "free-floating lines are 0.6pt minimum. (0.2 mm)" |
| W49 Supacolor blog / ProWorld FAQ (via summary) | "at least one point or 0.35 millimeters" |

- Verdict: about **1 pt (0.35 mm, 0.012 to 0.014 in)** minimum positive line for screen print: **ESTABLISHED** (W40, W43, W45, W49). Supacolor's 0.6 pt free-floating figure is a heat-transfer product and the outlier (CONFLICT within Supacolor's own pages).

### 7.2 Screen print: minimum negative gap (show-through, counters, knockouts)

| Source | Minimum negative gap |
|---|---|
| W40 Transfer Express | ".04″ thick for show-thru areas" (1.02 mm, 2.9 pt) for all inks except puff; puff 0.10 in. "Show-through spacing applies to all non-printing areas, including the open spaces within text characters." |
| W41 Transfer Express video | ".04" or around 2.8pts"; the reason is "closure", where ink spread fills details "like the interior of the letter O" |
| W45 Kotis Design | "1.5 pt negative space" (0.53 mm) |
| W44 FM Expressions | stand-alone white: 1 pt positive, "2 pt. ... (negative space)" |

- Verdict: negative gaps must be **larger than positive lines**: **ESTABLISHED** (W40, W44, W45 all set negative > positive). The number is a **CONFLICT**: 1.5 pt (Kotis, direct screen print) to 2.9 pt (Transfer Express, screen-printed transfers that are heat pressed, which adds spread). Safe audit default: **2 pt (0.7 mm) warn, 1.5 pt fail**; use 2.9 pt if the method is screen-printed transfer.
- Mechanism: plastisol and transfer inks spread on the fabric and spread again under heat and pressure, so open areas shrink and small counters (e, a, R, the inside of serif caps, gaps between diamonds and letters, the gap between a thin ring and adjacent text) close first (W41).

### 7.3 Screen print: halftones and dot gain
- W47 Anatol: minimum halftone dot 6 percent, maximum 97 percent (many cap in the upper 80s); 45 LPI for most halftones, 65 LPI photorealistic; mesh 230 to 280; tension at least 25 N/cm2.
- W46 Clark (GRAPHICS PRO): on garments keep halftones "somewhere in the 40-75% range"; below 40 percent dots get lost, above 75 percent reads as solid.
- Verdict: **CONFLICT** on the usable range (6 to 97 percent equipment capability versus 40 to 75 percent design advice). Both agree halftones shift on fabric. The seal is solid one-colour line art, so the audit rule is simply: **no halftones, tints, or soft edges** (supported by 7.7).

### 7.4 DTF: minimum positive line

| Source | Minimum line |
|---|---|
| W42 Clement / Stahls' (UltraColor MAX DTF) | "The minimum thickness required for DTF artwork is .018"" (0.46 mm, 1.3 pt) |
| W41 / Transfer Express search summary | UltraColor MAX ".018″ for printed areas" (same company as W42; not independent) |
| W55 Ninja Transfers (via summary) | "Elements smaller than 0.02 inches (0.5mm) may not transfer properly" |
| W52 T-shirt.ca | "lines and small details should be at least 0.5 mm thick" |
| W50 Kolormatrix | technical minimum 0.02 to 0.03 in (1.5 to 2 pt); production-safe 0.04 to 0.06 in (3 to 4.5 pt) |
| W51 Jiffy | "strokes below 0.8 to 1 millimeter are unreliable" |
| W53 Inkthreadable (neck labels) | "Minimum line thickness: 1 mm" |
| W56 Fourthwall (DTFx) | "Avoid text smaller than 1mm or 4pt font" |

- Verdict: **absolute DTF minimum about 0.5 mm (0.02 in, 1.4 pt)**: **ESTABLISHED** (W42, W52, W55, W50). **Production-safe 0.8 to 1 mm (2.3 to 2.8 pt) or more**: **ESTABLISHED** (W50, W51, W53). Audit default: **fail below 0.5 mm, warn below 1 mm**.

### 7.5 DTF: negative gaps, peeling, adhesion
- Gaps: W50 technical 0.02 to 0.03 in, production-safe 0.04 to 0.06 in; W53 "Minimum gap between elements: 1 mm"; W41/Transfer Express says UltraColor MAX has "no limit for non-printing areas (show-through)".
- Verdict on gaps: **CONFLICT**. Transfer Express says DTF has no show-through minimum; two other vendors set about 1 mm. Use **1 mm (0.04 in, 2.8 pt) warn** as the conservative default.
- Peeling mechanism, W51 Jiffy: "Very thin strokes can also break during peeling because they do not have enough adhesive mass to stay bonded to the fabric"; "Small shapes without enough surface area lose adhesion first ... the adhesive bond is weaker than the peel force"; stretching stresses narrow areas and causes cracks in wear. W50: thin lines "can break apart, lose powder coverage, or lift after pressing". W55 (via summary): press thin and separated elements longer and peel slowly.
- Verdict on the mechanism: **ESTABLISHED** (W50, W51, W55). Relevance to the seal: the small solid diamonds and isolated serif tips are exactly the "small shapes without enough surface area"; on fleece (DTF) they are the first things to lift.

### 7.6 DTF halftones
- W60 DIY Printz: "Most DTF jobs land around 45–65 LPI"; 35 to 45 for vintage texture. Dots that are too small "break up and look peppered".
- W61 Transfer Superstars: "a common range for DTF is between 45-65 LPI".
- Search summary (printedmint): 30 LPI at 300 dpi gives dots about 0.8 mm; adhesive powder needs a minimum dot size to stick.
- Verdict: 45 to 65 LPI **ESTABLISHED** (W60, W61). Not relevant to a solid seal except as a reason to avoid any tint.

### 7.7 DTG: minimum line and text
| Source | Number |
|---|---|
| W57 Threadless (DTG) | "Line weight should be no less than 2 pt thickness"; "Text works best when it is at least 0.25" or 18 points"; underbase "might not line up perfectly with smaller fonts or thinner lines which could lead to a slight ghosting effect" |
| W44 FM Expressions (full-colour digital) | "Full Color to be opaque on all colored garments: Line/area thickness 3 pt. or 0.04166 of an inch"; "Any areas that are thinner than 3 pt. will not be backed in white and will not appear opaque on dark garments." White outlines 1 pt |
| W54 Inkthreadable (DTG) | "keeping all elements no smaller than 10pt text"; DTG max area 32 x 50 cm |
| W58 Gooten (via summary) | lines or text "not be narrower than 10 pixels" (at 300 dpi that is about 0.85 mm, 2.4 pt) |

- Verdict: on a **dark garment with white underbase, keep lines at 2 to 3 pt (0.7 to 1.06 mm) or more**: **ESTABLISHED** (W57 2 pt, W44 3 pt, W58 about 2.4 pt). This is the strictest of the three methods and is the binding constraint for a white-on-navy seal if the jacket is DTG. Text: **CONFLICT** on minimum (10 pt W54 versus 18 pt / 0.25 in W57).
- Mechanism: on dark fabric DTG prints a white underbase first; thin features are either dropped from the underbase (W44) or misregister against it (W57), so they print thin, translucent or ghosted.

### 7.8 White ink on dark fabric: underbase, choke, halos
- Underbase needed on dark garments: W57, W59, W44, Printful glossary (seen in search). **ESTABLISHED.**
- Semi-transparent pixels: W59 Printify: a "30% transparent pixel" triggers "a 100% solid white dot underneath", giving "a weird, chalky halo". Search summary of the same Printify hub: "Your design should have exactly two kinds of pixels: 100% solid, or 100% transparent." **SINGLE SOURCE** with clear mechanism; consistent with W44. Audit rule: anti-aliased edges in a raster DTG file must be clean (vector or hard-edged 300 dpi PNG).
- Choke: screen-print forum threads (via summary) cite underbase chokes of about 0.75 pt; Printify (via summary) "Increase Choke slightly if you see halos". **SINGLE SOURCE / forum level**; a printer's job, not the designer's. For a one-colour white seal on navy, white is usually both underbase and top (or a single heavy white), so choke mostly matters for multi-colour work.
- Reversed (light on dark) weight: W14 Google Fonts: in print the dark ink spreads into light letterforms, thinning them; on screens light type looks heavier. W64 Boxcar (letterpress on paper): add "AT LEAST a 0.75pt extra stroke" to reversed designs. **CONFLICT in direction for our case**: on a garment, the white is the printed ink (positive), so it spreads outward and closes the navy gaps; it is not a knockout of dark ink. So for this seal, the risk is **closing counters and gaps**, not thin white strokes disappearing to spread. Do not blindly add stroke; check negative gaps (7.2, 7.5) after any thickening.

### 7.9 Minimum text size on garments (all methods)
- W50 Kolormatrix: technical 0.10 to 0.12 in tall (about 7 to 9 pt); production-safe 0.15 to 0.20 in tall (11 to 14 pt).
- W57 Threadless DTG: at least 0.25 in or 18 pt.
- W54 Inkthreadable DTG and W53 DTF: 10 pt.
- W56 Fourthwall DTFx: avoid below 1 mm or 4 pt.
- Search summary (generic screen print): 8 pt minimum; 6 pt for white reverse.
- Verdict: **CONFLICT** (4 pt to 18 pt). The overlap of the stricter sources is **about 10 to 14 pt (cap height about 2.5 to 3.5 mm)**. Audit default: **warn below 10 pt, fail below 7 pt** for serif caps, and require that the thinnest stroke inside a letter still clears the line minimum for the method (7.1, 7.4, 7.7). That stroke rule is the real constraint for small serif caps with hairline serifs.

### 7.10 Back print sizing
- W62 ScreenPrinting.com: full back "10"-14" wide, 6"-15" tall"; upper back 10 to 14 in wide, 1 to 6 in tall; "12" wide is a good rule of thumb for 13x19" film".
- W63 Printify: full back "10″-14″ wide and 6″-15″ tall", starting "three to four inches below the collar"; upper back about four inches below the collar.
- Search summary (generic): DTG standard max printable area about 12 x 16 in for adult tees. W54: DTG 32 x 50 cm (12.6 x 19.7 in). W45: screen pallet up to 15 x 17 in (XL 18 x 22). W42: DTF sheet up to 22 x 22 in.
- Verdict: **10 to 14 in wide full back ESTABLISHED** (W62, W63); 12 in is the common default (W62). A 10 or 12 in seal is within normal range. A 12 in seal on a small jacket may need the 10 in version (W62: platen adjustment for garment size).

### 7.11 What these numbers mean at our sizes
Not a source, a calculation for the audit. If the seal artwork is drawn at 1000 units across:
- at 12 in wide, 1 unit = 0.012 in = 0.30 mm = 0.86 pt;
- at 10 in wide, 1 unit = 0.010 in = 0.25 mm = 0.72 pt.
So the screen-print 1 pt line minimum is about 1.2 units at 12 in and 1.4 units at 10 in; the DTG 3 pt dark-garment line is about 3.5 units at 12 in and 4.2 units at 10 in; the 1 mm DTF safe line is about 3.3 units at 12 in and 3.9 units at 10 in. The audit should measure at the smaller (10 in) size, since it is the binding case.

## Topic 8. Golden rules (tests)

**8.1 Squint / blur test.**
- W65 NN/g: "Use the squint test to identify elements that catch attention and assess the visual hierarchy in your design."
- W66 Sketchplanations (credits Larry Leifer, Stanford): "Good designs give you a sense of the structure, even through a blurry lens."
- Polypane blog (via summary): primary element should be "the biggest, darkest, most obvious blob".
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED**. For the seal, the blurred image should resolve to: outer ring, a ring of texture (band), the top word, and the drawing, in that order of prominence; the diamonds and EST. 2023 should disappear.

**8.2 Design in black and white first / one colour.**
- W20 Airey (via summary): "No amount of fancy gradients or color choices will rescue a poorly designed mark. By refraining from using color until the end of the process..."
- W18 Rand: "reproducible in one color".
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED**.

**8.3 Five-second test.**
- W67 Kuric: show the design "for exactly five seconds. Then, you hide the picture and ask the participant a couple of questions"; origin Christine Perfetti, mid-2000s; checks recall of the main message or name.
- Second source: search summary of UX Psychology and Userlytics glossaries describes the same protocol.
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** as a method (it is a usability test with people; an automated audit can only approximate it, for example by asking whether the brand word is the first thing named).

**8.4 Consistency.** Covered by 5.1 (stroke), 4.1 (tracking), 3.3 (size scale). **ESTABLISHED** as practice.

**8.5 Remove until it breaks.**
- W70 Saint-Exupéry (Wind, Sand and Stars, via EdTech Books): perfection is attained "not when there is no longer anything to add, but when there is no longer anything to take away".
- W18 Rand: complexity "harbors a self-destruct mechanism".
- Verdict: **ESTABLISHED** as a principle (two sources), but it is a heuristic, not measurable. Audit form: list each element and ask whether removing it would lose meaning or recognition; the diamonds and the thin ring are the candidates in this seal.
