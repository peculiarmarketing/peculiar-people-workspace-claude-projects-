# Cross-cutting findings

Research date 3 September 2026. These are the questions that do not belong to any one platform.

## 1. Does cross-posting the same asset carry a real penalty?

This is the crux of the breadth question, so it gets the most evidence.

### What the platforms actually say

Meta published the clearest statement on 13 March 2026: it promotes "original content", defined as "videos or posts filmed or produced directly by the creator", and "content that is duplicative or involves minor edits to another creator's post will be considered unoriginal and deprioritized". Accounts that persist can have their "account deemed non-recommendable and demonetized" (about.fb.com, CONFIRMED).

Instagram's own creator guidance is more specific about mechanism. When it finds identical content, "the original content will directly replace the copied version in recommendations", and aggregator accounts posting ten or more copies within thirty days "will not be shown in surfaces where we recommend content" (creators.instagram.com, 30 April 2024). Content with visible watermarks from other apps is ineligible for recommendation.

**Read the language carefully, because the distinction it draws is the whole answer.** Every one of those rules is about content that is *someone else's*, or content carrying another platform's watermark. "Another creator's post." "The copied version." "Aggregator accounts." None of it describes a creator uploading their own file natively to two platforms.

### What a practitioner test found

Katie Steckly spent six months posting the same short-form videos to Reels, TikTok and Shorts and published the results (15 September 2025, 51,850 views). Her conclusion is to keep doing it. She recommends one strategy across all three.

Her only stated reason not to cross-post identically is not a penalty at all. It is format fit:

> "The type of thing that's kind of your bread and butter top performing content on Instagram and to some extent TikTok in my experience doesn't do as well on Shorts."

Specifically, B-roll plus trending audio plus text, the classic viral reel formula, performs well on Instagram, decently on TikTok and poorly on YouTube Shorts, because "YouTube Shorts viewers prefer a more YouTube style of short form content", meaning original, explanatory, tutorial or talking-head material. Otherwise the correlation holds: "If I had one video do really well on Reels or TikTok, I checked the other platforms and it did better than average there, too."

Erin On Demand corroborates the format-fit point independently: "almost all of the content that performs really well on Instagram does not perform as well on TikTok" (6 January 2026).

### The one thing that is genuinely unresolved

Marketers on Social Media Examiner (16 April 2026) walked through Meta's originality policy and raised a question they could not answer: does "original" quietly mean "original to Facebook"? One host suspected Meta does not want reposts from YouTube or TikTok. The other argued a video posted to Facebook for the first time should be fine regardless of where else it lives.

They did not resolve it, and neither can I. **UNKNOWN.** It is marketer speculation, not a Meta statement. The mechanism they describe, if accurate, is a content "fingerprint" that is pattern-based rather than facial, weighted by the page's own history: "If your page generally is uploading original content, then you're likely going to be in the safe zone."

### The answer

**Cross-posting your own original work, uploaded natively to each platform, with no third-party watermark, carries no documented penalty.** The rules that exist target reposting other people's work, aggregation, and visible watermarks. Two independent practitioner sources ran or observed the practice and recommend it.

**What is not free is format fit.** The same file will underperform on the platform whose audience wants a different register, and the specific finding is that Instagram-native trend content dies on YouTube Shorts. That is a reason to choose which platforms receive which asset, not a reason to stop cross-posting.

**Two operational rules follow, and both are already in the Posting Rota:** never upload a TikTok-watermarked file elsewhere, and never use the Instagram-to-Facebook cross-post toggle for anything that matters. Upload the clean file natively to each. The Rota's stated reason ("it compresses and strips") is right, and Meta's originality policy now gives it a second reason.

## 2. The marginal cost and marginal return of platform three, four and five

### What each extra platform actually costs per week

These are estimates built from the platform files, assuming the content already exists because the Posting Rota produced it. The distinction that matters is between platforms that *receive* an existing asset and platforms that *demand* their own.

| Platform | Marginal hours per week | What kind of cost |
|---|---|---|
| Instagram (primary) | 4 to 5 | Production plus 20 minutes daily commenting |
| Facebook Page | 0.2 to 0.3 | Receives, native upload |
| Pinterest | 0.5 | Receives, generated from existing assets |
| TikTok | 0.2 to 0.3 receiving, 4+ standalone | Receives, but wants daily native |
| YouTube Shorts | 0.2 | Receives |
| Threads | 0.2 to 0.3 | Receives text splinters |
| X | 0.1 receiving, 2 to 3 standalone | Wrong register, little to receive |
| LinkedIn | 2 to 3 | Demands its own content |
| Facebook Groups | 2 to 4 | Demands human attention, cannot be automated |
| Reddit | 2 to 4 | Demands months of history first |
| YouTube long form | 6 to 10 | Demands a third production pillar |

**The honest shape of this is not a smooth curve.** Platforms that receive an existing asset cost roughly ten to thirty minutes a week each. Platforms that demand native behaviour cost two to ten hours each. The marginal cost of platform three is nearly nothing if platform three is Facebook or Pinterest, and it is a second job if platform three is YouTube long form or Reddit.

**But the receiving cost is not the real cost.** Three hidden costs do not appear in that table:

1. **Comment obligation.** The Posting Rota commits to replying to every comment and message personally, and names that as "the single thing separating people who get value from social from people who do not". That obligation scales linearly with platforms, not with posts. Six inboxes is six inboxes.
2. **Judgement load.** Each platform has its own norms, its own failure modes and its own brand-safety context. The TikTok finding in this study, that #ldstemple is roughly half ex-Mormon content, is exactly the kind of thing that only shows up once you are already there.
3. **Attention fragmentation at the reading stage.** The Posting Rota's week-four review depends on ranking twenty posts by saves and shares to find the one pillar worth splintering hardest. Spread the same twenty posts across six platforms and each platform has three or four data points, which is not enough to read anything.

That third cost is the one that actually decides this. **Breadth does not just cost hours. It costs the ability to learn.**

### Is there a documented pattern of small brands winning by concentrating?

Yes, and the prior research already found three independent versions of it, which is why narrowing is the best-corroborated finding across four rounds. This round adds two more.

Erin On Demand's thesis, from a platform-by-platform breakdown made specifically to answer this question (6 January 2026):

> "The creators who are winning, they are not trying to win everywhere. They're also not trying to be everywhere. They are doubling down on the platform that makes the most sense for them."

And her close: "I'm not saying you have to be everywhere, but pick the one that makes the most sense for you."

The Playbook already recorded Trent Kennelly's critique of the GaryVee model naming "two to three platforms maximum" for a solo operator.

**Against that, one honest complication.** None of these sources are running controlled experiments. They are practitioners with a view. The concentration argument is well corroborated but it is corroborated by opinion, not by measurement, and it should be held as a strong prior rather than a proven law. The one thing that *is* measured, and that cuts the other way, is that cross-posting an existing asset carries no penalty. So the strongest defensible position is not "be on one platform". It is **produce for one, distribute to several, and only count the ones you are willing to read.**

## 3. Credibility versus reach: two different reasons to open an account

Evan asked for these to be answered separately, and they genuinely are separate, because they lead to different amounts of work.

### The reach argument

An account exists to put content in front of strangers. It requires posting, cadence, replying, and reading the results. It costs hours every week forever. It only makes sense where the audience is, where the format fits, and where the brand can win.

By that test, this study supports very few platforms. Instagram and Facebook have the audience. Pinterest and YouTube have the search memory. Facebook Groups has the audience already gathered and sorted. Everything else fails on audience size, format fit, or both.

### The credibility argument

An account exists so that a person who searches for the brand finds something rather than nothing. It requires a claimed handle, a profile photo, a bio, a link, and perhaps three posts. It costs an hour once and nothing thereafter.

This is a real effect and a practitioner named it precisely (Erin On Demand, 6 January 2026):

> "Nine times out of 10, a brand is going to go to your Instagram and kind of just check it out just to kind of get a snapshot of who you are. And not even just brands, but like potential customers ... I'm always like wait what's her Instagram so I can see, like, is she my vibe?"

For Peculiar People this matters more than for most brands, because the readiness board says the store has zero reviews, zero testimonials and no photograph of a real person wearing the product. A buyer being asked to pay $44.99 with no social proof will look the brand up. An empty result is a data point against buying.

**But the credibility argument has a hard limit that the reach argument does not, and it is the thing that surprised me most in this study: half the platforms are invisible to a logged-out visitor.**

Verified on 3 September 2026:

| Platform | What a logged-out searcher sees |
|---|---|
| Instagram | Content, logged out. Works. |
| TikTok | Content behind a prompt. Mostly works. |
| YouTube | Everything. Works. |
| Etsy | Everything. Works. |
| Snapchat | Profiles and Spotlight. Works. |
| Facebook | Partially, varies. |
| **Pinterest** | **Redirected to an empty shell.** |
| **Threads** | **Login wall.** |
| **X** | **Hard login modal.** |
| **LinkedIn** | **Sign-in page.** |
| **Reddit** | Blocked to my tools entirely. |

**Claiming a handle on X, LinkedIn or Threads does not buy credibility, because the person checking cannot see it without an account.** That collapses the "be everywhere for credibility" argument for exactly the platforms it is usually used to justify. The credibility case survives only for Instagram, YouTube, Facebook and Etsy, and of those, Instagram is where people actually look.

**The practical consequence:** claim the handles everywhere to protect the name, which is a defensive trademark action costing an hour. But do not confuse that with a credibility strategy. The only accounts that do credibility work are ones a stranger can actually read.

## 4. The owned audience alternative: email and a blog

The Playbook already recorded Seth Godin as "the serious dissent", the argument that social platforms turn you into their unpaid employee, and noted that he reaches roughly a million blog readers using none of them. It also recorded him as the third independent source telling Evan to narrow. That deserves more than a nod, so here it is weighed properly.

### The case for it

**It is the only channel where the brand owns the relationship.** Every platform in this study can change its rules, and several did in the last twelve months: Instagram reach fell 31% to 35%, TikTok views fell 31%, LinkedIn impressions fell 23%, and Facebook reach rose 51%. A business built on any one of those is a business whose distribution is somebody else's product decision.

**The data supports the conversion argument, in direction if not in magnitude.** Salesforce's Q2 2026 Shopping Index found social driving 29% more ecommerce visits year over year while order volume rose 1% and cart abandonment sat at 82%. More traffic, converting worse. I discarded the widely quoted "email converts at 4 to 5.6%" figures because every source I traced for them was an SEO content farm with no primary data, so **the specific claim that email outconverts social is UNKNOWN by this study's evidence standard.** The direction, that social traffic is getting less valuable, is CONFIRMED.

**A blog is the one asset that compounds and that the brand owns outright.** Evan has 119 temples of source-verified research and a documented body of knowledge on which official sources serve the wrong building. As product-page copy, that material is doing a fraction of the work it could. As indexed pages on peculiarpeopleco.com, each one is a durable answer to "when was the Manti Temple dedicated" that works for years and sends traffic to a store the brand controls.

Note what the platform files found about search memory: only Pinterest, YouTube long form and Reddit have it, and all three hand the compounding to somebody else. A blog is the same property, owned.

**A practitioner independently reached the same place.** Erin On Demand's list of six platforms included Substack, for exactly this reason: "you own your audience contact. So this is email ... it's not like social media where if your social media gets hacked, you're just doomed."

### The case against it, honestly

**It is even slower than Pinterest, and the brand's constraint is now.** BRAND.md section 16 is blunt: zero orders ever, distribution is the only constraint. A blog that ranks in nine months does not produce a first customer this quarter. Godin's million readers are the output of decades of daily posting, not a strategy a pre-launch brand can adopt.

**Email has a denominator problem.** There is no list. An email strategy with zero subscribers is a social strategy in disguise, because something has to bring people in. The Playbook already shelved Klaviyo flows until roughly 50 orders exist, correctly.

**It cannot do the credibility job.** A stranger checking whether a brand is real looks at Instagram, not at a blog.

### The weighing

**The owned-audience argument is right about the destination and wrong about the sequence.** Every criticism Godin makes of platform dependence is correct and applies fully here. But a brand with zero orders does not have an audience to own yet, and the cheapest way to acquire the first few hundred people is the interest-media feeds, precisely because they reach strangers without a follower base.

The resolution is not to choose. It is to make the social work feed the owned asset from day one. Concretely, and none of this is new work:

- The temple facts already written for product pages should also exist as indexed pages on peculiarpeopleco.com. That is a copy-and-publish operation on material that exists, and it is the single highest-leverage thing in this section.
- Email capture should exist on the store before the first campaign, so the traffic that social generates is not spent once.
- The bulk-order inquiry form BRAND.md section 9 already approved is an email capture in disguise, aimed at the highest-value segment.

**One warning that follows from this study's own findings.** BRAND.md section 12 records that the research library's value depends on facts being tiered and verified, and that "an omitted fact is invisible, a wrong fact is a review". Publishing that library as indexed web pages raises the stakes on that discipline, because a blog post is permanent and searchable in a way an Instagram carousel is not. That is an argument for doing it carefully, not for not doing it.

## 5. What changes with two operators instead of one

Both operating documents were written for one person and say so. The Posting Rota opens by promising "no models, no budget, no face, and nothing that needs anyone else's permission". Here is what actually changes and what does not.

### What genuinely changes

**The comment obligation becomes survivable across more than one platform.** The single hardest constraint on breadth is the promise to reply to everything personally, plus twenty minutes a day of proactive commenting. That is roughly four hours a week on one platform. A second person does not double the platforms that are viable, but it makes two viable where one was.

**Facebook Groups becomes possible.** This is the biggest unlock. Groups is the largest verified Latter-day Saint audience found anywhere in this study, at 267K, 162K, 45K, 44K, 37K, 36K, 28K, 22K and 21K members in public groups, including two temple groups and an art group. It is also the only surface that cannot be automated, batched or splintered. It costs pure human attention. That is exactly the resource a second operator adds and no tool provides.

**Photographs of real people become available**, which BRAND.md section 19 already identifies. Still blocked on the garment decision.

**Production and publishing can separate**, which BRAND.md section 17 already sketches: Evan supplies the research pillar and the sample deliveries, Bailee runs production, publishing, comments and the week-four read.

### What does not change

**Two people is not a team, and the volume advice still does not apply.** The Playbook established that every high-volume example (Hormozi's 450 pieces a week, GaryVee's 397 a day) is a team clipping long-form recordings. Two people with day jobs is not that. BRAND.md section 19 already says not to raise the five-then-nine cadence before four clean reps of each show exist, and nothing in this round changes that.

**The reading problem does not go away.** Two operators post more, but the week-four review still needs enough posts per platform to tell signal from noise. Two people spreading across six platforms have the same learning problem as one person spreading across six platforms.

**Neither operator should be assumed willing to appear on camera**, so TikTok's and YouTube's core demands remain unmet regardless of headcount.

### The decision a second operator forces

**Facebook Groups makes the attribution question urgent, and this is the concrete finding.** BRAND.md section 19 lists "voice and attribution once Bailee runs social" as unresolved and says it should be settled before the first post. Group posting is done from a personal profile, not a brand Page. A post in "LDS temples" carries a real person's name and photograph to 22,000 people. There is no brand-neutral way to participate in a Facebook group.

So the moment Facebook Groups enters the plan, the answer to "whose 'I' is speaking" stops being a preference and becomes a fact visible to the audience. That decision should be made before, not after.
