# TikTok

Research date 3 September 2026.

## 1. Who is actually there

**United States.** 37% of US adults use TikTok, up from 33% in 2024 and 21% in 2021 (CONFIRMED, Pew, fielded 5 February to 18 June 2025). 24% of all US adults use it daily (Pew, separate survey, n=5,123, fielded 24 February to 2 March 2025). TikTok's ad tools reported 153 million US adult users, 55.7% of adults, in October 2025 (DataReportal, 5 November 2025). CONFLICT with Pew, same ad-reach inflation as elsewhere; I take Pew.

**Age, US, June 2025 (Pew, CONFIRMED).** 18-29: 63%. 30-49: 44%. 50-64: 30%. 65+: 12%. Daily use by age: 47% of 18-29s, 28% of 30-49s, 17% of 50-64s, 5% of 65+.

**Sex, US, June 2025 (Pew, CONFIRMED).** Women 42%, men 30%. TikTok's own ad tools reported the opposite globally, 52.3% male, which reflects a global user base rather than a US one. Keep those separate.

**Global.** TikTok is private and publishes no user count. Ad-reach estimates only.

Read against BRAND.md section 10: TikTok reaches the returned-missionary avatar well and the gift-buying-women avatar acceptably. It reaches the 50+ grandmother buyer poorly.

## 2. How a post reaches someone who does not follow the account

**Interest media, and the purest example of it.** CONFIRMED. TikTok's own documentation states the recommendation system "selects from a large collection of eligible content and ranks them based on the system's prediction of how likely you'll be interested in each one", driven by user interactions, content information and user information (support.tiktok.com, current).

I verified the practical version first hand on 3 September 2026: **@shopzmci, an LDS modest-clothing and temple-dress brand with 169 followers, had a post surfacing in the #ldstemple tag** next to accounts many times its size. That is the single most directly relevant observation in this whole research round, because 169 followers is roughly Evan's starting position.

The decisive ranking signal, per a practitioner who ran a six-month test, is average watch time and whether someone watched the full video (Katie Steckly, 15 September 2025, 51,850 views). SINGLE SOURCE.

## 3. Shelf life

Days, occasionally weeks. TikTok resurfaces older videos more readily than Instagram, but there is no durable long tail. UNKNOWN in precise terms.

## 4. Searchability

Better than Instagram, worse than YouTube or Pinterest. TikTok functions as a search engine for a younger cohort and hashtag pages are public. Google indexes some TikTok content. But a temple name typed into TikTok next April will not reliably find a post from this September. UNKNOWN in measurable terms.

## 5. Link behavior

A bio link only for most accounts. No clickable link in a video caption. Same structural weakness as Instagram.

## 6. Native commerce, including TikTok Shop specifically

This is the open question BRAND.md and the Playbook both flagged. Here is what I can and cannot resolve.

**Is the category allowed? Yes. CONFIRMED.** I read both governing policies directly, each effective 1 September 2026. The TikTok Shop US Prohibited Products Policy does not list religious items as a prohibited category. Its only religion-adjacent clause bars "products promoting discrimination based on protected attributes" including religion, which is the opposite of what Evan sells. The TikTok Shop US Restricted Products Policy does not mention religious items, religious imagery, faith-based products, or custom and print-on-demand apparel at all. **This corrects a claim in circulation that religious products are barred.** A UK and EU version of the policy does class products "inappropriate according to certain local cultures or social conventions (e.g. religious or political-related elements)" as prohibited, but that is not the US policy and Evan sells to the US.

**Is there a buyer base for Latter-day Saint temple art on TikTok Shop? Still UNKNOWN, and I do not think it is knowable from public sources.** TikTok publishes no category sales data. What I can offer is a proxy and a caution.

The proxy: a genuinely comparable business exists on Etsy. PaperTempleArt of Dallas, Texas sells per-temple art at $80 for a shadow box and $150 for a custom one, and has 113 sales in 16 years on Etsy with a 5.0 rating from 28 reviews. Premium temple art sells. It does not sell in volume.

The caution: the Comfrt and Bloom mechanism the Playbook admired works because creators earn commission from TikTok directly and keep posting for free. That requires a supply of creators who want to post about your product. See section 11 for what the LDS creator pool on TikTok actually looks like, because it is not a neutral pool.

Meta has now built its own version. Facebook rolled out affiliate tools where creators self-serve products from a brand catalogue and attach affiliate links to reels, described by practitioners as "a play for TikTok marketplace" (Social Media Examiner, 16 April 2026). If the creator-commission mechanism is what appeals, it is no longer TikTok-only.

## 7. What it demands, and can Evan meet it

9:16 vertical video, filmed vertically from the start, text on screen from frame one, first second decides everything. Creator account rather than business account. Daily or near-daily posting: Erin On Demand's read is that TikTok "demands frequency" and the people doing well post "every day if not multiple times a day" (6 January 2026). SINGLE SOURCE but consistent with everything else.

**Partly met.** The Inspection is native to TikTok. Temple Study as a slow pan over line art with text works. Carousels can be reposted as photo posts. What Evan cannot bring is the thing TikTok rewards most, which is personality on camera, and BRAND.md is explicit that neither operator should be assumed willing to appear.

## 8. What it is bad at

- **Reach is collapsing.** Metricool's 2026 TikTok Study (2,314,756 posts, 92,000+ accounts, January to February 2025 versus January to February 2026) found video views down 31.30%, reach down 28.73% and interactions down 31.17%, while regular videos published rose 72.10% and image posts rose nearly 140%. Twice the supply chasing two thirds of the attention. SINGLE SOURCE, vendor data, but large sample.
- **Virality does not convert into community.** "Going viral on the platform does not mean that you are building a real community" (Erin On Demand, 6 January 2026). SINGLE SOURCE.
- **No link in a post.**
- **It is the wrong format for the brand's best asset.** Evan's differentiator is a 119-temple verified research library. That is text and images. TikTok is a video-completion machine.
- **Brand adjacency risk, and this is the serious one.** See section 11.

## 9. Real cost per week

If TikTok only receives video already produced for Instagram, the marginal cost is roughly ten to fifteen minutes a week: export a clean unwatermarked file and upload natively. Practitioners who cross-post do it manually and consider it worth the time (Katie Steckly, 15 September 2025).

If TikTok is treated as a primary platform demanding daily posting, it is a different job entirely and well beyond what two people with a finished pipeline and no audience should take on.

Going quiet for a month costs nothing structurally. Reach is per-post.

## 10. Rules and account risk

TikTok Shop policy is covered above. On organic posting, religious content is unrestricted. The Posting Rota's existing rules already handle the platform-specific traps: never post a TikTok-watermarked file to Reels, and set the account up in the country you are selling to.

## 11. Is there a Latter-day Saint audience here today

**Yes, and it is the most hostile environment I found.** Verified logged out on 3 September 2026 by reading the #ldstemple tag, which rendered 116 items.

Faithful content is present: @jordan_mcclanahan ("Yes I'm a Mormon convert"), @kiarra.hillman, "D A Y L E, LDS Convert", "Godissogood", @aris_johring, "Come Follow Him", "The Church Is True Podcast", plus Portuguese-language content. Notably "Tia Smith" posts "Temple Tip" videos on temple symbolism "most members never notice", which is the Temple Study format already running.

But roughly half the tag is ex-Mormon and critical content, sitting in the same recommendation pool:

- @vegas.ex.mormon: "after going through the temple it shifts to being super weird ... #exmormon #mormon #ldstemple #mormontemple #cult"
- @miracleofforgiven: "Who are these people and what are they doing? #cult #highcontrolreligion"
- @genxldsl: "#lds #mormon #ldstemple #ldschurch #exmormon"
- @exmo_exlds
- "Blame it on Mel", explaining temple garments to outsiders
- @rosejohnsonnnn: "#mormon #wedding #ldstemple #unworthy"

Instagram's version of the same tag showed none of this. I looked at both on the same day with the same query.

This is a TikTok-specific finding and it matters more for this brand than for most. Peculiar People is unaffiliated (BRAND.md section 14), must never imply affiliation, and must never take a political or combative position (section 13). Posting reverent temple content into a tag where the adjacent video calls the Church a high-control religion puts the brand in a conversation it has explicitly decided not to enter, in front of an audience that is partly there to argue. The comments the Posting Rota promises to answer personally would be a different job on TikTok than on Instagram.

Commercially: @shopzmci ("modest clothing and temple dresses", Utah, links Shopzmci.com) has 169 followers and 1,978 likes. #ldsapparel exists as a tag but did not render logged out, so its size is UNKNOWN. TikTok user search is gated, so I could not size accounts.
