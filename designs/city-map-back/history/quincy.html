Quincy, Illinois

The Town
Settled in 1821 on limestone bluffs above the Mississippi, and the Adams County seat from 1825. In the winter of 1838 to 1839 it had about 1,500 people by one Church count and 1,600 by another.

The Exiles
More than 5,000 Latter-day Saints driven out of Missouri came to Quincy that winter. Thousands walked east across the frozen river. Townspeople took families into their homes, found them work, and gathered food, clothing and money.

Upriver
Quincy was a stopping place. Within about a year most of the Saints had moved some 45 miles north to Commerce, which became Nauvoo.

What Happened Here

  • Mid-February 1839: Emma Smith, with Joseph still jailed in Missouri, crossed the frozen Mississippi on foot with her four children. Under her skirt, in cotton bags a friend had sewn, she carried the manuscripts of Joseph's Bible translation. She found lodging with Judge John Cleveland and his wife, Sarah, about four miles from town.
  • February 25 to 28, 1839: The Democratic Association of Quincy met over several days, named a relief committee chaired by J. W. Whitney, and passed resolutions condemning Missouri's treatment of the Saints. The committee set out to feed, clothe and house at least 5,000 people.
  • April 22, 1839: Joseph Smith stepped off a ferry at Quincy after escaping custody in Missouri, in a wide-brimmed hat with his collar turned up. Dimick Huntington recognized him on the bank and took him by back streets to the Cleveland place, where Emma ran out and met him halfway to the gate.
  • May 4 to 6, 1839: A general conference at a Presbyterian camp ground just outside town approved the land purchases at Commerce and the Twelve's mission to Europe. Joseph and his family left for Commerce on May 10.
  • June 28, 2002: In the area for the Nauvoo Temple dedication, President Gordon B. Hinckley and the Tabernacle Choir gave Quincy a benefit concert. It raised $75,000 to help the city start a community foundation.

On the Map

  1. Washington Park, on Maine Street between 4th and 5th, holds a marker the Church and the Illinois State Historical Society placed in 1976. It says many of the Saints who crossed into Illinois at Quincy "were made welcome by the people here."
  2. Clat Adams Bicentennial Park sits on the riverfront. In May 2025, for the county's 200th anniversary, the Church unveiled a rendering of its "Mercy on the Mississippi" panel for the park. Its background painting shows Saints huddled on the shore, waiting to cross the icy river.

Trivia

  • Eliza R. Snow's poem "To the Citizens of Quincy" ran in the Quincy Whig on May 11, 1839, addressed to the town's "Sons and Daughters of Benevolence."
  • Quincy got its own stake on October 25, 1840. It was cut back to a branch the next year.
  • Once the ice broke up, people crossed in skiffs, canoes and other small boats until the ferries opened for the season. Some families on the Missouri side waited as long as two weeks for the river to thaw enough to cross.
