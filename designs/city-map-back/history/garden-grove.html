Garden Grove, Iowa

Who It Was For
Partly for people who had not arrived yet. Church leaders built the camp, in Orson Pratt's words, "for the benefit of the poor" who couldn't travel any farther, and for the poor still coming behind them from Nauvoo. It was the first of the permanent camps on the road across Iowa.

Halfway Across
About halfway across Iowa, on the Weldon Fork of the Grand River. Brigham Young and Henry G. Sherwood picked the spot.

What Happened Here

  • April 24, 1846: The lead wagons of Brigham Young's company stopped here. Orson Pratt wrote that they "came to a place which we named Garden Grove."
  • April 27, 1846: A horn called the men together to be organized for work, and the rain soaked them while they were at it. Within three weeks they had broken 715 acres of prairie sod. By May 10 Orson Pratt thought the place looked as if it had "been occupied for years."
  • May 10, 1846: Garden Grove was organized as a branch, with Samuel Bent as president and David Fullmer and Aaron Johnson as his counselors. Bent was told to divide the land according to need. Brigham Young and the main camp moved on west on May 13.
  • August 16, 1846: Samuel Bent died at Garden Grove. Between 500 and 600 Saints spent the next winter there, and as many as 70 of them died before spring.
  • 1852: The camp kept supplying wagon trains until the last of the Saints left for the Salt Lake Valley. Only a few families stayed behind.

On the Map

  • The pioneer cemetery is on Mormon Trail Road, just west of town, in a small county park looked after by the Decatur County Conservation Board. It is open to the public.
  • The town of Garden Grove grew up next to the old camp. The settlement site itself is private farmland nearby.

Trivia

  • Hosea Stout, who got there a few days after the work started, wrote that the farm went up so fast it seemed a magic city of the woods.
  • The April 27 work list survives in Orson Pratt's journal. One hundred men were put to cutting trees, splitting rails and making fence, and forty-eight to cutting logs for houses. Others dug wells, built a bridge, made wood for the ploughs and herded stock, and some were sent several days' journey into the Missouri settlements to trade horses and feather beds for cows.
