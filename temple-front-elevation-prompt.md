# Temple line-drawing prompt, front elevation (jacket backs)

Adapted from `temple-sketch-prompt.md` for a flat, straight-on front elevation
instead of a three-quarter view. Paste the whole block below alongside a
photograph of the temple's front taken as close to head-on as possible, plus the
style reference drawing. The line marked PER-TEMPLE is the only part that
changes between temples; fill in the counts read off the reference photo before
running.

What changed from the three-quarter prompt: the drawing is an orthographic
elevation, so perspective from the photo is removed rather than copied; every
horizontal is truly horizontal and the horizontal overrun tails left and right
are called out as a signature feature and must never mirror left to right; and the counted-elements paragraph
counts the facade's stacked windows instead of a receding colonnade.

---

Convert the attached photograph of this temple into a loose architectural esquisse in front elevation: a designer's freehand concept sketch of the building's front face, seen perfectly straight on, in pure black ink line on a pure white background, the building centered with generous white margin on all sides. Reproduce this exact building faithfully from the photograph: its massing, proportions, tower and spire configuration, roof and parapet shapes, window placement and rhythm, and entrances. Every mark is a pen stroke; nothing is filled, shaded, toned, or hatched. The entire drawing is boldly inked with a broad felt-tip or brush pen rather than a fine liner, so even the lightest lines are substantial, clearly visible marks and the heaviest read as thick, weighty bands, and the whole sketch prints strong and graphic rather than delicate.

The view is a true front elevation, not a copy of the camera's angle. Draw the facade as flat and orthographic, the way an architect draws an elevation: every vertical edge perfectly vertical and parallel, every horizontal edge perfectly horizontal and level, no converging lines, no vanishing points, no foreshortening, and no side walls or roofs visible beyond what is seen when standing dead centre in front of the building. If the photograph was taken from below or slightly off centre, so that the towers lean inward or one side looks narrower than the other, correct it: straighten the verticals, level the horizontals, and make the facade symmetrical wherever the real building is symmetrical. Correcting perspective never changes a proportion; it only removes the camera's distortion.

Proportions are taken from the photograph and this paragraph outranks every composition rule later in this prompt. Before drawing, register the building's key ratios directly from the photo: how many times taller than the main walls the towers and spires stand, how far above the main roofline the topmost element rises, how the heights of the towers compare to each other, and how wide the whole facade spans relative to the wall height. Carry those exact ratios into the drawing unchanged. Whatever is tall in the photograph is equally tall in the drawing; whatever is slender stays equally slender; whatever is long and low stays long and low. Towers and spires are the most commonly distorted elements: never shorten a tower, thicken a spire shaft, or make any vertical element stubbier, blockier, or more compact than the photograph shows. If anything, err toward the towers reading slightly taller and thinner than the photo, never shorter and wider. A photo taken from ground level makes high elements look shorter than they are, so when straightening the perspective, let the upper towers keep their full height. Never compress the building vertically or squash its massing to fit the frame, to leave room for margins, or to suit the pen; if space is tight, scale the entire drawing down uniformly and keep every ratio intact. Any style reference images govern stroke character, line weight and overrun behaviour only; every proportion, ratio, and dimension comes from the photograph alone, and nothing about the building shapes in the reference drawings transfers.

Peaks and points are closed. This rule outranks every other rule in this prompt except the two paragraphs above. Wherever two lines converge to a point, whether the apex of a spire, steeple, pinnacle, turret, gable, buttress cap, or finial, the two strokes travel up, meet, and stop exactly at the tip. Nothing whatsoever continues past a peak: no overshoot, no tail, no guide line, no draft line, no construction line, no bar, no mark of any kind above, through, or beside it. Every spire is a clean unbroken triangle rising into blank paper. The sky around and above each spire and tower is completely empty white, and no line passes in front of, behind, or across a spire shaft at any height along its rise.

Overrun every junction where two lines meet and both keep going. Window corners, door corners, wall corners, parapet and battlement corners, roof-to-wall junctions, buttress and tower setback edges, band intersections, feature corners, base corners: at each of these, each line runs visibly past the meeting point, leaving open overshooting tails, so rectangles read as four long strokes overlapping near their corners rather than closed shapes. This applies only to junctions where lines genuinely pass one another; it never applies to a converging point or apex, which always terminates cleanly. Interior detail lines overrun just as universally as structural ones. Every window corner, door corner, sill end, band, cornice, mullion, and subordinate feature edge crosses and continues past its neighbours in the lightest weight, as small, crisp, crossed tails, dense across the whole facade, so the wall surfaces read as a field of overlapping strokes rather than tidy closed rectangles. The peak rule above governs only the tapering silhouette of spires and pinnacles; it never suppresses these small interior crossings anywhere else on the building, including high on the towers.

Tails that shoot beyond the building's silhouette into the open white are very long and decisive, drawn at the medium weight: a thick, substantial stroke, clearly bolder than the interior detail lines and about half the thickness of the heavy structural outline, laid with the same broad pen and never thinning to a scratch as it travels. In a front elevation the horizontal tails are the signature of the drawing. The horizontal lines that reach the outer edge of the facade, meaning the wall base, the plinth, the string courses and horizontal bands, the sill lines that meet the outer wall, the parapet and battlement lines, the tower shoulders, cornices and setbacks, and the bases of pinnacles, continue straight out past the silhouette into the white, perfectly level, in the exact line of the edge that made it. These horizontal tails appear at many heights of the building, top to bottom, so the facade is flanked by a stacked field of long level strokes. Vertical edges overrun too: the wall corners and tower edges drop as long vertical tails below the wall base, and where a tower or wall edge meets a parapet or setback partway up, it may continue a short way upward into the white beside the next stage, but never alongside the tapering part of a spire. Tails are long: the longest horizontal tails reach out from the silhouette by about a third of the building's width, many others reach a fourth, and only a few are short stubs, so they read as confident strokes carrying real distance into the white rather than small nicks at the edge. The longest vertical tails drop below the wall base by about a fifth of the building's height.

The tails are never symmetrical, even though the building is. Draw the left side's tails and the right side's tails as if by two separate, unplanned passes of the hand; nothing on one side is a mirror or echo of the other. At any given height, the left tail and the right tail are clearly different lengths, often one long and one short, and at many heights a tail exists on one side only, with nothing on the other. Across the whole drawing, one side carries more long horizontal tails than the other. The same holds for vertical tails below the base: each drops its own length with no two alike, a long one is never matched by an equally long one at the mirrored position across the centre line, and some edges drop no tail at all while their mirror partner drops a long one. Tails are never arranged in pairs, steps, a regular rhythm, or evenly spaced rows; their lengths and spacing look irregular and spontaneous, like a real designer's quick sketch. None travels so far that it nears the edge of the page.

One exception, and it applies only to the spires themselves. Above the springing line where a spire, steeple, or pinnacle begins to taper toward its point, no tails are generated at all: the two converging edges of that cone or pyramid run cleanly to the tip with nothing branching off them, nothing overshooting the tip, and nothing crossing the shaft from either side. Horizontal tails elsewhere may be long, but none of them is aimed so as to pass through or over a spire; the tapering portion of every spire sits in untouched white from its springing line to its point.

Line hierarchy, three clearly separated weights in a fixed ratio of roughly 5 : 2.5 : 1. HEAVIEST, five units: the full outer silhouette of the building and every major structural edge, meaning the outer edges of each tower, the edges where a tower meets a recessed wall, the parapet and roofline, the spire edges, and the base edge of the walls, all drawn at one uniform heavy weight regardless of direction. These are the thickest marks on the page, about one hundredth of the image width, reading as bold weighty ribbons of ink so the entire structure registers at a glance from across a room. MEDIUM, two and a half units: the overrun tails that extend past the silhouette into the surrounding white space, a full, substantial stroke, half the thickness of the heavy structural outline and well over twice the interior detail lines, never a thin scratch or a tapering hairline. LIGHTEST, one unit: all interior detail lines, meaning window and door openings, horizontal bands, cornice lines, battlement notches, subordinate feature edges, and the overrun tails that stay inside the silhouette; these are the thinnest lines in the drawing but still firm, confident strokes at roughly one five-hundredth of the image width, clearly visible from a distance, never faint hairlines. The three weights must be unmistakably distinct from one another at a glance.

Stroke character: confident, decisive strokes, but never mechanically uniform. Each line varies in thickness along its length, swelling and thinning slightly, or tapering gradually toward its ends, so every stroke has its own organic silhouette, like ink laid by hand with changing pressure. This variation is subtle and never breaks the weight hierarchy: a heavy line at its thinnest point is still clearly heavier than a medium line, and a medium tail at its thinnest point is still clearly heavier than an interior detail line. Not shaky or wobbly, just alive. Lines that the elevation makes vertical or horizontal stay true to that direction along their whole length; the life is in the weight, not in wandering.

Detail rules. Draw window and door openings as simple open outlines in the lightest weight with overshooting corner tails. If the photograph shows a mullion or major dividing bar inside a window, draw it as a single light stroke that may run slightly past the window frame; if the photo shows none, leave the window empty. A few small touches of draftsman's detail are welcome, always in the lightest weight: a short sill stroke beneath each window that runs slightly past the jambs on both sides; a second inner line following the curve of any arched opening or arched window head; cornices and horizontal trim bands drawn as two closely spaced parallel lines rather than one; battlements drawn as a simple notched line following the photo's rhythm; and along two or three of the heaviest silhouette edges anywhere on the building except the tapering portion of a spire, a second searching stroke laid slightly off the first, as if the hand tested the line before committing. Never add more than this: no textures, no repeated tick marks, no invented ornament the photograph does not show. Never draw fine glazing grids, small individual panes, or resolved tracery, and never fill a window or door with solid black. Never draw brick, stone coursing, shingle lines, siding, railing pickets, lettering on inscription panels, or any repeating surface texture; all wall and roof surfaces stay empty white. Omit all landscaping and surroundings: no trees, shrubs, flowers, lawns, paths, fences, gates, pools, fountains, lampposts, flagpoles, cars, people, or neighbouring buildings. The bottommost edge of the building, where its walls and plinth terminate, is always drawn, in the heaviest weight, running the full width of the structure: this line belongs to the building itself and is never omitted. Below that edge there is no ground line, horizon line, site line, or shadow of any kind; the building floats on open white paper, and the only marks beneath the wall base are the vertical overrun tails dropping from the building's own corners and edges, plus the horizontal tails of the base line itself running out left and right. No text, lettering, signatures, labels, or watermarks anywhere. Crisp black on white, no paper texture, no border, no background tone.

Counted elements. Wherever the photograph shows a run of repeated parts on the facade, such as the windows stacked up a tower, the round windows in a recessed bay, the doors at the base, the pinnacles ringing a tower stage, or the bays across the front, count them in the photograph and draw exactly that many, no more and no fewer, at the same heights relative to one another. Count each tower and each bay separately; in a symmetrical facade the left and right sides carry the same counts. This count is a proportion fact taken from the photograph and outranks any impulse to simplify or to fill the available height. PER-TEMPLE: for the Salt Lake Temple east front, each outer tower has five tall arched windows stacked one above another; each recessed bay between the towers has, from top to bottom, a round window, a tall arched window, a second round window, and an arched entrance door at the base; the central tower has an arched inscription panel near the top (drawn empty, no lettering) above two tall arched windows stacked one above the other; the outer towers end in tall spires topped with a ball and finial, and the central tower ends in a taller spire topped with a ball and the standing Angel Moroni figure.

Each steeple or spire is topped exactly as shown in the photograph, whether a finial, ball, weathervane, standing figure, or plain bare point, and nothing else. A standing figure may be drawn as a small solid black silhouette, the one solid mark allowed in the drawing. The topmost mark of every tower is that element itself; no extra shape, bar, stroke, or line is added at, above, or near the top of any spire, steeple, pinnacle, or tower.

Composition and framing: the building sits squarely in the middle of a square frame, its vertical centre line aligned with the centre of the image and equal white margin left and right, and the facade drawn symmetrically about that line wherever the real building is symmetrical. Vertically, place it so the margin above the topmost spire tip or figure is a little larger than the margin below the lowest hanging tail, giving the towers room to rise. Margins are measured from the outermost overrun tails, not from the walls: every tail, in all four directions, falls comfortably inside the frame with clear white beyond it. Nothing is cropped, clipped, or touches an edge: no spire tip, no figure, no finial, no tail end. The building is never pushed to one side, and no part of the composition is weighted or offset for effect. These margins are achieved only by scaling the whole drawing uniformly, never by shortening a tower, compressing the building vertically, or altering any proportion stated above.
